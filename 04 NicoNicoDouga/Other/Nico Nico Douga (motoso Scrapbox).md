# Nico Nico Douga

A video community service.

> [Akihiko Koizuka](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E6%88%80%E5%A1%9A_%E6%98%AD%E5%BD%A6). ["The Creativity of Nico Nico Douga: Five Years of the Video Community Service 'Nico Nico Douga'", *Jōhō Shori*](https://ipsj.ixsq.nii.ac.jp/ej/index.php?action=pages_view_main&active_action=repository_action_common_download&item_id=81712&item_no=1&attribute_id=1&file_no=1&page_id=13&block_id=8) (Journal of the Information Processing Society of Japan). 2012, vol. 53, no. 5, pp. 483–488.[^tn-koizuka]

> I feel like it's about time I properly wrote about how Nico Douga's business model was designed. I've written about it in bits and pieces, but I don't think I've ever explained it all in one place....

> [@gweoipfsd](https://x.com/gweoipfsd/status/1804498483623375105): Next, since I've talked many times about Nico Douga's comeback after being cut off, if I write something I haven't said before, I think it'll centre on the fact that the basic strategy for Nico Douga's revival was actually to turn Niconico into a religion, to make it like Apple users. What moves we made to turn it into a religion.[^tn-source]

Recollections by [Kawango](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E3%81%8B%E3%82%8F%E3%82%93%E3%81%94)[^tn-kawango]

> The first trigger was a short video, less than ten seconds long, that [Hiroyuki](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E3%81%B2%E3%82%8D%E3%82%86%E3%81%8D) posted just after New Year 2007. I think *Yūkan Fuji* or some paper ran an article saying Hiroyuki's car had been seized or something? Hiroyuki, saying he didn't even own a car, posted a joke video of a toy car, "it's been seized". Looking back, it was a damn boring video, but the title alone, "seized", was funny, and I think it got about 70,000 views. That was a new record for Nico Nico Douga at the time. I think there were hardly any videos with even 10,000 views.[^tn-toycar]

Related: [Hiroyuki's unpaid damages](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E3%81%B2%E3%82%8D%E3%82%86%E3%81%8D%E3%81%AE%E8%B3%A0%E5%84%9F%E9%87%91%E8%B8%8F%E3%81%BF%E5%80%92%E3%81%97)

> From that day on, traffic surged.

> For several days in a row it practically doubled every day. Back then, when I was interviewed for an article about how hugely popular Nico Douga was, a few days after the interview they sent me the article to check it for errors.

> I remember answering, about the site's daily page views, "Sorry, it's more than double that now."

> Now, after the explosion of the site in January and the coverage of it, something happened.

> The [VIPPERs](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/VIPPER) came back.[^tn-vipper]

> Some people see Nico Douga's and 2channel's user bases as continuous, but from the operators' point of view it's a bit different.

> When Nico Nico Douga (Kari) opened in December 2006, the core 2channel users called VIPPERs invaded right away, because Hiroyuki was the administrator. They spent about a week [trolling](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E8%8D%92%E3%82%89%E3%81%97) Nico Nico Douga mercilessly and then left.

> I thought, what useless, annoying people.

> What they left behind was a deserted Nico Nico Douga. Then, as I said, the Nico Nico Douga boom happened after New Year. The VIPPERs, who I think learned from an ITmedia article that Nico Nico Douga was getting talked about, went, "Nico Nico Douga? Wasn't that our colony?", suddenly claimed ownership and came back to Nico Nico Douga acting as if they owned the place.

> In fact, our access analysis showed that the drivers of the Nico Nico Douga boom were internet beginners. Their search engine was almost always Yahoo, not Google. There were also a great many mixi users. But the biggest group was users who used no internet service other than Nico Nico Douga.[^tn-yahoo]

> In fact, the first Niconico user I met outside the company wasn't an acquaintance from the web industry. The person who told me about Nico Nico Douga, saying there was this damn hilarious site, was an employee of [avex](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E3%82%A8%E3%82%A4%E3%83%99%E3%83%83%E3%82%AF%E3%82%B9), Dwango's largest shareholder at the time. That was when I became sure Nico Nico Douga was really going to take off.

It crossed the [Chasm](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/Chasm).[^tn-chasm]

> But because Hiroyuki was the administrator and the public face, a two-layered structure had formed within two months of Nico Nico Douga's launch: people like the VIPPERs, the internet's core users, mistook Nico Nico Douga, which had been made lively by internet beginners, for their own site.

> This had a huge influence on how Nico Nico Douga's culture formed afterwards. I think most core internet users didn't notice this two-layered structure at all until the first Chōkaigi. And I think even after Chōkaigi, the majority of users didn't believe it.[^tn-chokaigi]

> Now, the first boom born of Nico Douga's explosion in January 2007 was the chorus. Every night, users appeared who sang Remioromen's "[Konayuki](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E7%B2%89%E9%9B%AA)" in comments on Nico Nico Douga.[^tn-konayuki]

> The chorus spread from "Konayuki" to all sorts of other anime songs. I think this was the influence of the VIPPERs flowing back in.

> And it was in February 2007, the fateful third month of Nico Nico Douga, that it really became talked about like a social phenomenon.

> In just one month, February 2007, Nico Nico Douga became a social phenomenon and then suddenly vanished, and that would give birth to the myth that followed.

> This was also around when the avex employee told me about a site called Nico Nico Douga.

> Rumours began spreading online that more than half of YouTube's traffic was coming via Nico Nico Douga.

> Around this time, a phenomenon began in which the top 20 or so of Nico Nico Douga's rankings occupied, almost unchanged, the top 20 of YouTube's world rankings. The top of YouTube's world rankings was filled with videos of incomprehensible Japanese anime art; not even videos, really, often just a single still image with music, with nothing in the description but "for saving to Nico Nico Douga". At the time Nico Nico Douga was [a service that put comments on top of YouTube videos](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/Youtube%E3%81%AE%E5%8B%95%E7%94%BB%E3%81%AE%E4%B8%8A%E3%81%AB%E3%82%B3%E3%83%A1%E3%83%B3%E3%83%88%E3%82%92%E8%BC%89%E3%81%9B%E3%82%8B%E3%82%B5%E3%83%BC%E3%83%93%E3%82%B9), so Niconico users thought of [YouTube](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/Youtube) as little more than a storage place for videos to post on Nico Nico Douga. Then foreign YouTube users, angry and wondering what on earth had happened, discovered Japan's Nico Nico Douga and raided it.

> They started trolling the comments in English. Niconico users fought back, and a battle began to overwhelm them with sheer volume of comments and push the English spam comments off. Of course, the Niconico users won by a landslide.[^tn-comments]

> I remember, late one night back then, chatting through comments with one user who was working hard to add fresh Japanese comments to a video that still had spam comments on it.

> Seeing the user proudly report, "We managed to drive them off," I thought at that moment: no, it won't end like this.

> I had a feeling we'd be cut off by YouTube soon, and from the next day I started running around securing bandwidth for after a YouTube cut-off.

> But, as expected, a DDoS attack on Nico Douga soon began. I assumed it was from angry overseas YouTube users, but it turned out that at almost the same time, YouTube had cut Nico Douga off.[^tn-cutoff]

> It was the moment everyone on the Japanese internet thought the Nico Nico Douga boom had been forcibly ended.

> That was fun, wasn't it. We knew it couldn't last long, but it ended sooner than expected. Just as the internet was overflowing with voices like that, Dwango approved the business decision to relaunch Nico Douga as a video site of its own.

> I asked President Kobayashi at the time, "[I'll definitely make it succeed, so get ¥1 billion ready for me](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E7%B5%B6%E5%AF%BE%E3%81%AB%E6%88%90%E5%8A%9F%E3%81%95%E3%81%9B%E3%82%8B%E3%81%8B%E3%82%8910%E5%84%84%E7%94%A8%E6%84%8F%E3%81%97%E3%81%A6%E3%81%8F%E3%82%8C)." President Kobayashi, thinking ¥1 billion wouldn't be enough, quietly got ¥2 billion ready. "If this fails, Dwango goes under," the two of us laughed. And in the end it cost ¥4 billion.[^tn-kobayashi]

I love this part[^tn-kokosuki]

> It was the start of Nico Nico Douga (γ). In truth, Nico Nico Douga's real history begins with the revival after the YouTube cut-off.

> That's it for today.

> As I was writing I remembered that I wrote a similar text on the operators' blog ages ago, around the end of a year or the start of one. By the way, I think for roughly the first two years of Nico Nico Douga I wrote nearly every word of the operators' blog and the press releases myself.

> The story of Nico Nico Douga's revival after the YouTube cut-off.

> I'm writing from memory, so there may be mistakes.

> Nico Nico Douga came back one week after the service was shut down by the YouTube cut-off. In that one week, almost all the important policies on the new Nico Nico Douga's specification, concept and promotion were decided.

> There were an insane number of things to decide, and even looking back, almost every decision was right. It was a godlike week, even across my whole life.

> Well, I've talked about parts of this here and there. What I haven't talked about is Nico Nico Douga's hidden promotion strategy: turning it into a [religion](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E5%AE%97%E6%95%99).

> Originally, Nico Nico Douga (Kari) was literally "provisional": a service that doubled as test marketing, and we even hid the fact that Dwango was running it. If it looked like it would work, the plan was to reveal that it was actually Dwango's first web service, change the name to something cool, present it to the world afresh and launch the service. So that both we and the users could throw the name away without regret when it changed, we decided to give it the most uncool, awful name possible so nobody would get attached to it. That's how we settled on the silly name "Nico Nico Douga". But Nico Nico Douga became a huge hit almost instantly, so we lost the chance to change the name. If anything, both we and the users were starting to like this awful name nobody was supposed to get attached to. It was a miscalculation.[^tn-name]

> Now there was nothing for it but to charge ahead under the Nico Nico Douga brand. We decided to say the name had been calculated from the start, as irony and an antithesis to all the web services out there with try-hard names, and so the name Nico Nico Douga (γ) was decided too.

> Yes, the truth is, at first we felt like, let's give ours a clever, cool service name too, like the usual internet services, say "Google". At first.

> But it couldn't be helped. Now that it had come to this, I thought, the only way to explain the [fundamentally wrong branding](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E6%A0%B9%E6%9C%AC%E7%9A%84%E3%81%AB%E9%96%93%E9%81%95%E3%81%A3%E3%81%A6%E3%81%84%E3%82%8B%E3%83%96%E3%83%A9%E3%83%B3%E3%83%87%E3%82%A3%E3%83%B3%E3%82%B0) that is "Nico Nico Douga" was to take a position as a new set of values that picks a fight with, mocks and sneers at the whole Japanese internet industry, ruled as it is by Google and Google-worshipping values.

> Actually, at first we'd meant to be let into the club of those pretentious web services, you know. At first.

> It's all the fault of the name Nico Nico Douga.

> Half out of desperation, we decided to make deifying Nico Nico Douga, that is, turning it into a religion, the basic strategy of our promotion.

> There were two models for the religion I had in mind at the time: [Jun'ichirō Koizumi](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E5%B0%8F%E6%B3%89%E7%B4%94%E4%B8%80%E9%83%8E)'s "theatre politics" and the [Apple faithful](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%83%E3%83%97%E3%83%AB%E4%BF%A1%E8%80%85) that Jobs created. I came up with an internal catchphrase: Niconico would be a "[theatre-style web service](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E5%8A%87%E5%A0%B4%E5%9E%8B%E3%82%A6%E3%82%A7%E3%83%96%E3%82%B5%E3%83%BC%E3%83%93%E3%82%B9)". I often make internal-only catchphrases so the staff share a service's concept. At Niconico back then, besides "theatre-style web service", there was also "[humanity's last rebellion against the Google machine empire](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/Google%E6%A9%9F%E6%A2%B0%E5%B8%9D%E5%9B%BD%E3%81%B8%E3%81%AE%E4%BA%BA%E9%A1%9E%E6%9C%80%E5%BE%8C%E3%81%AE%E5%8F%8D%E4%B9%B1)" and so on. I think there were others, but I've mostly forgotten them.[^tn-koizumi]

> I'd long thought that among IT companies, Apple was the only one whose users had become believers, and whether the same thing could be done was a theme of mine for years. When Niconico was cut off by YouTube, I thought, right now, we could turn it into a religion.

> Because several conditions were in place. These three:

> ・ A story to serve as the basis for a myth.

> ・ In the relationship between operators and users, a situation where we could keep the product, that is, access, in short supply.

> ・ The overwhelming level of media interest in Niconico at the time.

> I'll explain them in order.

> In less than a month, Nico Nico Douga's users had exploded until it was roughly one of Japan's major internet services, and then, cut off by YouTube, it shut down. For it to rebuild the service from the hardship of its users dropping to zero and take on YouTube again is already a well-made story. And it actually happened. What's more, this story already has woven into it a history of the persecution and suffering of the faithful, which is very important to a myth.

> And we expected there were potential users wanting to access Nico Nico Douga in Japan on the order of several million. But on our own bandwidth we could only provide the service to about 100,000 people for the time being. In business, to create believers, it's essential that the product is scarce and hard to get. In short, the scarcity business model matters. Ramen shops, high-end brands, concert tickets: they all use scarcity deliberately. But for internet services, if people can't access the service, users just leave, so scarcity basically doesn't work. With Nico Nico Douga at the time, though, we genuinely didn't have enough bandwidth, so, exceptionally, we had no choice but to run on scarcity. And there was no service other than Nico Nico Douga where you could comment on videos.[^tn-scarcity]

> And finally, there was the overwhelming media interest. Ever since my salaryman days I've kept launching new businesses and have always written press releases. Even now I often check them. And there are two things I strongly believe about press releases. One is that however hard you work on them, only a tiny fraction get turned into articles. The other is that rival companies check press releases when they become articles, so you often get copied.

> Around when we started the ringtone business, I realized that for internet services it was enough to inform users on the site, and so for [really important new services we don't put out press releases](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E9%87%8D%E8%A6%81%E3%81%AA%E6%96%B0%E3%82%B5%E3%83%BC%E3%83%93%E3%82%B9%E3%81%AF%E3%83%97%E3%83%AC%E3%82%B9%E3%83%AA%E3%83%AA%E3%83%BC%E3%82%B9%E3%82%92%E6%89%93%E3%81%9F%E3%81%AA%E3%81%84); we announce them quietly on the site. I came to use press releases differently: for things that look important to the world, meaning the media, but aren't that important for the business, things we don't mind rivals copying, or, better still, things where it'd be lucky if a rival misunderstood and copied them and wasted their resources.

> When Nico Nico Douga became something like a social phenomenon online, that changed. Things we announced only on the site, without press releases, kept getting picked up by the media and reported as news. I tried all sorts of things to see how to avoid being reported, but I only remember the conclusion: [when something is really the talk of the town, nothing you do to avoid being reported works](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E6%9C%AC%E5%BD%93%E3%81%AB%E4%B8%96%E3%81%AE%E4%B8%AD%E3%81%A7%E8%A9%B1%E9%A1%8C%E3%81%AB%E3%81%AA%E3%81%A3%E3%81%A6%E3%81%84%E3%82%8B%E3%81%A8%E3%81%8D%E3%81%AF%E3%80%81%E5%A0%B1%E9%81%93%E3%81%95%E3%82%8C%E3%81%AA%E3%81%84%E3%82%88%E3%81%86%E3%81%AB%E4%BD%95%E3%82%92%E3%82%84%E3%81%A3%E3%81%A6%E3%82%82%E7%84%A1%E9%A7%84); it gets reported anyway.

> Conversely, I thought that if we used this media attention, we could control the information we put out and do our branding well. [Information control](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E6%83%85%E5%A0%B1%E3%82%B3%E3%83%B3%E3%83%88%E3%83%AD%E3%83%BC%E3%83%AB) is the basis of [branding](https://scrapbox.io/motoso/%E3%83%96%E3%83%A9%E3%83%B3%E3%83%87%E3%82%A3%E3%83%B3%E3%82%B0). Artists restrict what they release into the world, even a single photo. They only let photos with the image they want out into the world. That's how branding is done.

> So, I thought, with Nico Nico Douga as it was then, we could probably turn it into a religion and make the users believers. When the service resumed in March 2007, the basic policy was decided: turn Nico Nico Douga into a religion, build a community of believers again, and use that energy as the driving force to take on YouTube.

> This has got long, so what we actually did will be in the next post.

> [!info] Cultural context and translator's notes
> This is a page from the personal Scrapbox (wiki notebook) of a user called motoso, titled after the project 基素基. Apart from a citation, a few link words and two short remarks ("It crossed the Chasm", "I love this part"), it consists of quotes from Nobuo Kawakami ("Kawango"), Dwango's founder and the driving force behind Nico Nico Douga. The linked X post dates the quotes to around June 2024, more than seventeen years after the events, and Kawakami himself warns that he is writing from memory. The page stops at his promise of a "next post", which is not quoted here.
>
> It is the operator's side of the story that the vault's other sources tell from the users' side, and the accounts don't always agree:
> - **2channel users.** Kawakami says the VIPPERs trashed (Kari) and left, and that internet beginners drove the boom. [[Nico Nico Douga and 2channel (Part 1-1) - The Roots of Nico Users in the (Kari) Era]] and [[Nico Nico Douga and 2channel (Part 1-2) - Mixing and the Spread of 2ch Culture]] (December 2007) credit 2channelers with shaping the service. Kawakami gave the same short version in 2016: [[Kawakami Nobuo Looks Back on Ten Years of Nico Nico Douga]].
> - **The name.** Here it was chosen to be deliberately awful and disposable. In the 2007 developers' blog cited by Amata, it was "laid-back", decided in five seconds and liked by Hiroyuki.
> - **The toy-car video.** Kawakami remembers about 70,000 views. The quote in Part 1-2 has it reaching number two in YouTube's worldwide daily ranking.
> - **Dates.** The cut-off and suspension came around 23 February 2007, and (γ) opened on 6 March: two weeks, not Kawakami's "one week" ([[NicoNico - JP Wiki]], [[History of 2channel (Monapedia)]]).

[^tn-koizuka]: Akihiko Koizuka (戀塚昭彦) is the Dwango engineer who built Nico's comment system; see [[Nico Nico Douga and 2channel (Part 1-1) - The Roots of Nico Users in the (Kari) Era]]. His 2012 article in the IPSJ journal is a primary source on the first five years.
[^tn-source]: The first two quotes are Kawakami's own preview of the thread that follows. The linked X account is the source given on the page.
[^tn-kawango]: かわんご (*Kawango*) is Nobuo Kawakami's long-standing online nickname, a blend of "Kawakami" and "Dwango".
[^tn-toycar]: This is the same video described in [[Nico Nico Douga and 2channel (Part 1-2) - Mixing and the Spread of 2ch Culture]]. The real story was the creditor's attempt to seize the 2ch.net domain, reported by *Yūkan Fuji* on 12 January 2007 (see [[History of 2channel (Monapedia)]]); Kawakami misremembers it as a car.
[^tn-vipper]: VIPPER: regulars of 2channel's News Flash (VIP) board, the most active culture board at the time. See [[News Flash Board VIP]].
[^tn-yahoo]: In 2007 Yahoo! Japan was by far Japan's most-used portal and search engine, so "Yahoo users, not Google users" is Kawakami's shorthand for mainstream, non-technical users. mixi was Japan's dominant social network.
[^tn-chasm]: A reference to Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm*, which describes the gap between early adopters and the mainstream market. This line is motoso's comment on the quote above it.
[^tn-chokaigi]: Niconico Chōkaigi, Niconico's big offline event at Makuhari Messe, first held in April 2012. Kawakami's point is that the event's huge, mainstream crowd showed core users that they were a minority on "their" site.
[^tn-konayuki]: The "Konayuki" chorus, comments typed in unison with the song's chorus, is also Kawakami's example in [[Kawakami Nobuo Looks Back on Ten Years of Nico Nico Douga]].
[^tn-comments]: Comments on a Nico video are capped in number and the oldest drop off, so posting many new comments literally pushes old ones off the video. That is how the "battle" could be won by volume.
[^tn-cutoff]: Compare [[Nico Nico Douga and 2channel (Part 1-2) - Mixing and the Spread of 2ch Culture]], where the DDoS attack runs for three days before the 23 February suspension and YouTube's block comes "around the same time".
[^tn-kobayashi]: Hiroshi Kobayashi (小林宏) was Dwango's president at the time. ¥1 billion, ¥2 billion and ¥4 billion are roughly $8.5, 17 and 34 million at 2007 exchange rates.
[^tn-kokosuki]: ここすき (*koko suki*, "I love this part") is a Niconico-era reaction phrase, here motoso's own comment on the quote.
[^tn-name]: ニコニコ (*niko-niko*) is the onomatopoeia for smiling, so the name is literally "Smiley-Smiley Video". 動画 (*dōga*) means "video".
[^tn-koizumi]: Jun'ichirō Koizumi, Prime Minister 2001–2006, was famous for "theatre politics" (劇場型政治 *gekijō-gata seiji*): turning policy fights into a media drama with heroes and villains. Kawakami's "Google machine empire" slogan fits his long-running public opposition to Google-style automation.
[^tn-scarcity]: 品薄商法 (*shinausu shōhō*, "scarcity marketing") is the usual Japanese term for deliberately keeping supply short. The ~100,000-account cap and the months-long registration wait are described from the user side in [[Nico Nico Douga and 2channel (Part 1-2) - Mixing and the Spread of 2ch Culture]].
