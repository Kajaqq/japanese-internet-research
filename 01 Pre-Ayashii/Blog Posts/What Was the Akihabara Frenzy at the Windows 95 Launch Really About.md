# What Was the Akihabara Frenzy at the Windows 95 Launch Really About?

It has been 30 years since the Japanese edition of Windows 95 (for PC/AT compatibles and the PC-98) went on sale on 23 November 1995.[^tn-date][^tn-pc98]

It's fair to call it a landmark OS. It offered a GUI and multitasking with a decent level of usability and practicality on the personal computers (PCs) of the day. Backed by its overwhelming market share, it also brought unification: until then, software had not been compatible from one PC maker to the next, but the OS absorbed those differences so that the same software ran on any maker's hardware. In doing so, it laid the foundation of the Windows that continues to this day.

One thing that often comes up in talk about Windows 95 is that Akihabara back then was like a festival, and there's the story that [more than 20,000 people had gathered for the countdown to midnight on the 23rd](https://pc.watch.impress.co.jp/docs/news/731864.html).[^tn-pcwatch] Many people may also remember it being covered in a big way on the TV news. But **how credible is this story, really**?

The thing is, it's possible that **the people gathered there were almost all industry insiders, such as PC makers, Microsoft itself and the media, and that far fewer members of the public actually bought Windows 95 in Akihabara that day**.

Why can I say that? The FM TOWNS was a PC built on a Japanese domestic architecture, and it too lost its reason to exist when Windows 95 arrived.[^tn-fmtowns] Its dedicated magazine, *Oh! FM TOWNS*, carried in its January 1996 issue (it ceased publication with the following issue)[^tn-ohfm] a report on what Akihabara was like at the time[^tn-missing] and an article on what Windows 95 was like to use, and the content is interesting.

The following is quoted from the article "Special Feature II: The Japanese Edition Arrives — Start with Windows 95" (by 守男)[^tn-author] in *Oh! FM TOWNS*, January 1996, pp. 100–102.

> Pushing and shoving our way along, we found quite a line even at the Tsukumo Denki main store on the way.[^tn-tsukumo] Both kinds of shop were open late into the night and selling the Windows 95 package for about the same price, yet some had nobody going near them while others, like this one, had a long queue.
>
> "I'll have to look into what makes the difference later,"
>
> I said, and from the back right in front of me came a sour mutter:
>
> "Heh. The ones behind us are press too."[^tn-ushiro]
>
> The crowd around us was overwhelmingly made up of people obviously from shops and makers, wearing Microsoft's blue caps and staff jackets, and of reporters like us. The share of ordinary people seemed quite small. I heard later that NEC had gone all out, sending four buses and 125 people to Akihabara alone.[^tn-nec] Fujitsu, whose fluorescent-yellow staff jackets caught the eye here and there, also brought 1,000 FMVs preinstalled with Windows 95 and mobilized around a hundred people, red Touch Ojisan bags in hand.[^tn-touch] Behind a certain shop someone was even overheard saying, "If the press asks, tell them we had a lot more people out. We can't lose to NEC." On top of that there was Toshiba in blue jackets, and Apple people parading all over with placards reading "What Windows 95 makes possible, Macintosh already did more than ten years ago."[^tn-apple]
>
> *(omitted)*
>
> After killing time aimlessly, T and I checked the early-morning TV. We learned the power of TV editing.
>
> "Narration" → "Crowds from around 11 p.m." → "Countdown and fireworks" → "Line of people" → "Clerks briskly opening cardboard boxes packed with Windows 95"[^tn-typos] → "Windows 95 packages passing from hand to hand" → "Payment at the register" → "Amazake handed out by female staff"[^tn-amazake] → "Crowds"
>
> ...Seen this way, it can only look as though Windows 95 was flying off the shelves in the middle of the night. But as I wrote above, most of the people actually there were insiders and press. Some bought one as a memento, but the real big sales were probably at the weekend. (In fact, what that special program focused on most was the line for a rock-paper-scissors tournament with ¥10,000 gift certificates as prizes.)[^tn-janken]

To sum up the article: a single PC maker mobilized people to Akihabara by the hundreds, several makers did the same, and there is testimony that staff were told to give the press a bigger number than were actually mobilized. That may be why the event has been passed down, as mentioned above, as if a crowd of tens of thousands had gathered. Industry insiders and press made up the crowds in front of the PC shops, and filming just a few people buying Windows 95 was enough for it to be reported as if huge numbers had come to buy it.

The news that crowds turned up (not that they bought) for the midnight sale of this "revolutionary product" was the best advertising imaginable, and it fed strongly into the spread of PCs in Japan and into Windows 95's sales. People who didn't live through it may find this hard to believe, but beyond the news itself, current-affairs programs ran specials explaining what Windows 95 even was, and there were stories of middle-aged men who didn't own a PC trying to buy the much-talked-about Windows 95 at electronics stores.[^tn-ojisan] Yet the reality may have been nothing more than a fiction built by the PC makers. Still, in every era, the essence of selling things has been how to sell off a magic product that doesn't really exist without telling an outright lie. Today, you could say the same of AI.[^tn-ai]

So I've quoted this article, but I'd like more testimony. Is there anyone out there who was in Akihabara right after Windows 95 went on sale, as someone in the industry at the time, or who bought Windows 95 at a shop? I'd like to check how credible this story is, just to be sure.

> [!info] Cultural context and translator's notes
> This is a personal blog post on Hatena Blog, published on 2025-11-24, the day after the 30th anniversary of the Japanese launch. It is an argument, not a history: the whole case rests on one eyewitness report, the *Oh! FM TOWNS* article, and the blogger openly asks readers for more testimony. Keep in mind that the reporter wrote for a magazine whose platform Windows 95 was making obsolete, and that the magazine folded weeks later. A sardonic view of the launch is to be expected from that position. The report itself also admits that some people did buy a copy that night, and it expects real sales at the weekend. What it describes is a staged crowd, not a flop.
>
> For the opposite view in this vault, see [[A History of Japanese Internet Culture and Incidents, As I Saw It (1969–2018)]]. It treats the midnight crowds as fact and embeds release-day footage of Akihabara, which can be checked against the TV sequence the magazine breaks down here. See also [[Internet Old-Timers' Club - My First JUNET]] and [[The Internet - Nicopedia]] on Windows 95 as the moment the internet went mainstream in Japan.
>
> Hatena's automatic keyword links (`d.hatena.ne.jp/keyword/...`), added by the platform and not by the author, have been removed. The author's own link to PC Watch is kept. The two images are the blogger's scans of the magazine pages being quoted. Small typos in the source (a stray bracket, a broken link, a wrong particle) are fixed silently; those that affect meaning are footnoted.

[^tn-date]: 23 November 1995 was a Thursday and a national holiday in Japan, Labor Thanksgiving Day (勤労感謝の日 *Kinrō Kansha no Hi*). Shops started selling at 0:00 on the 23rd, hence the midnight countdown. The US launch was on 24 August 1995, so Japanese buyers had watched the hype from abroad for three months. The "weekend" in which the magazine expects the real sales would be 25–26 November.
[^tn-pc98]: Japan's PC market was split. NEC's PC-9800 series (PC-98) dominated, and its hardware was not compatible with IBM PC/AT compatibles, which spread in Japan from the early 1990s once DOS/V let them display Japanese without special hardware. Microsoft therefore shipped separate Windows 95 editions for the two platforms. This is the "unification" the author credits Windows 95 with, but it is a simplification. Windows 3.1 and DOS/V had already started the shift, and Windows 95 finished it.
[^tn-pcwatch]: The linked PC Watch piece is a 2015 report on a reunion marking the launch's 20th anniversary, not a contemporary report. It only says the crowd is *said* to have exceeded 20,000 (2万人を超えたとも言われる), and it does not mention a countdown, name any shop or give sales figures. So the blogger's own source for the "20,000" legend is itself secondhand and hedged.
[^tn-fmtowns]: The FM TOWNS (1989) was Fujitsu's multimedia PC. It used x86 processors but had its own proprietary architecture and Towns OS, and was known for its built-in CD-ROM drive and its games. Once Windows made hardware differences invisible to software, a platform like this had no selling point left. Fujitsu moved to its PC/AT-compatible FMV line (from 1993), which is why Fujitsu staff are promoting FMVs in the report.
[^tn-ohfm]: *Oh! FM TOWNS* was published by SoftBank (then Nihon SoftBank) as one of its "Oh!" platform magazines; it was renamed from *Oh! FM* in 1991. One reference source gives its last issue as the February 1996 issue, published in January 1996, which matches the blogger's "ceased with the following issue". A successor, *FMV Family*, appeared in April 1996. This rests on a single source, so verify before citing.
[^tn-missing]: The clipped text reads 当時の の状況 ("the situation of ___ at the time"), with a word missing. It was almost certainly 秋葉原 (Akihabara), which Hatena had turned into a keyword link that the clipper dropped. "Akihabara" is supplied in the translation.
[^tn-author]: 守男 is the byline of the magazine article, apparently a pen name. The reading is uncertain (possibly *Morio*), so it is left in kanji.
[^tn-tsukumo]: Tsukumo Denki (九十九電機, "Tsukumo") is a long-established Akihabara electronics and PC parts retailer; the 本店 (*honten*) is its main store. The writer's question is why queues formed at some late-opening shops and not at others selling at the same price. The implication is that queues followed the companies' mobilized staff, not prices.
[^tn-ushiro]: うしろさん (*ushiro-san*) means roughly "the people behind (us)". The person ahead in the queue overhears the writer say "I'll have to look into that later", realizes they're a reporter, and mutters sourly that the people behind are press as well. The joke is that even the queue is made up of insiders, and the person grumbling is presumably one of them.
[^tn-nec]: NEC had the most at stake. Windows 95 weakened the software lock-in that had kept the PC-98 dominant, so a large, visible presence at the launch was a way for NEC to be seen leading the new era, not losing to it.
[^tn-touch]: Touch Ojisan (タッチおじさん, "Touch Uncle") was a mascot character in Fujitsu's FMV advertising from about 1994 to 2000, used to sell the FMV as easy to use for people who were bad with machines. The Japanese is ambiguous about whether the 1,000 FMVs were carried by the hundred staff (unlikely) or were stock Fujitsu brought for the event; the translation assumes the latter.
[^tn-apple]: The Macintosh launched in 1984, so in 1995 its GUI was "more than ten years" old. Apple's mockery of Windows 95 as a catch-up to the Mac was a well-known counter-campaign at the time, and Apple ran similar jibes in its US advertising.
[^tn-typos]: The source has 定員 (*teiin*, "capacity/quota") for 店員 (*ten'in*, "shop clerk"), an IME conversion error in either the magazine or the blogger's transcription. Later in the same paragraph, 移されていた ("were moved") is a similar error for 映されていた ("were shown on screen"). Both are translated with the intended meaning.
[^tn-amazake]: Amazake (甘酒) is a sweet, warm drink made from fermented rice and handed out in winter at shrines and events. Here it was a welcome giveaway for people queuing outdoors at midnight in late November.
[^tn-janken]: A janken (rock-paper-scissors) tournament is a common Japanese prize game at shop events. The writer's point is that the long queues TV showed as "Windows 95 buyers" were mostly people lining up for a chance at ¥10,000 in gift certificates.
[^tn-ojisan]: The story of people without a computer buying Windows 95 because it was in the news is a famous anecdote in Japan about the 1995 boom, often retold as proof of how much the hype outran understanding. The blogger presents it as hearsay ("there were stories"), not as a documented fact.
[^tn-ai]: The aside was written in November 2025. It compares the 1995 hype to the marketing of generative AI.
