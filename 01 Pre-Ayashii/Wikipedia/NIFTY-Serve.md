# NIFTY-Serve

> [!note]
> This is a translation of the Japanese Wikipedia article *ニフティサーブ*. The notes in boxes like this one are additions by the translator and are not part of the original text. Where the article links to another Wikipedia page, I have linked to the matching note in this vault, and left plain text where there is none. The article's own notes and sources are kept at the bottom. Dates and "current" statements are those of the captured article.

**NIFTY-Serve** (ニフティサーブ, later **NIFTY SERVE**) was a PC-communications service run by Nifty Corporation from 1987 to 2006.

> [!note]
> *PC communications* (パソコン通信, *pasokon tsūshin*, often *pasotsū*) means the closed dial-up services of the modem era, where members dialled into one company's host computer, as opposed to the decentralised Internet. [[The Internet - Nicopedia]] and [[Internet Old-Timers' Club - My First JUNET]] explain the difference. NIFTY-Serve matters to this vault because [[Ayashii World Hub|Ayashii World]] began on its Home Party service in 1995, after Shiba had run a Patio there (see [[#Patio (PATIO)]], [[#Home Party (HP)]] and [[#Ayashii World]] below). The note on NIFTY in [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] is a short summary of this article.

## History

### Launching the service

By 1984 the American CompuServe had established itself as the world's largest network community, and several companies had the idea of building a Japanese version of it. Around that time Mitsubishi Corporation, ASCII and others approached CompuServe, but CompuServe had no intention of expanding outside the English-speaking world; its attitude was that it might license its operating know-how. On top of that, the licence fee CompuServe demanded was high, so nothing came of it. In 1985, Fujitsu and Nissho Iwai (now Sojitz) agreed to split the licence fee. Mitsui & Co. was already negotiating for the licence, but after negotiations with CompuServe it was Fujitsu and Nissho Iwai that received it.

On February 4, 1986, Nissho Iwai (NI) and Fujitsu (F) each seconded seven employees and set up a joint venture, **N.I.F.** (NIF). Full service began the following year, on April 15, 1987. Officially, N.I.F. is said to stand for Network Information Forum. [^1] The name of the communications service, NIFTY-Serve, combined N.I.F. and CompuServe. Because of this relationship, N.I.F. also became CompuServe's agent in Japan.

NIFTY-Serve licensed know-how from CompuServe: its command system and screen layout, online sign-up with credit-card payment, the drafting of the membership terms, and a community model in which the management of forums is delegated to system operators.

The host system was newly developed rather than taken from CompuServe. From the start, instead of the mainframes made by its parent company Fujitsu, it used several general-purpose UNIX workstations (machines with the MC68000, and later SPARCstations using SPARC). For access from ordinary public telephone lines it used FENICS (Fujitsu Enhanced Network for Information and Communication Services), the packet-switched network (VAN) service Fujitsu had built.

So that it could be reached from any terminal, the communications protocol was "non-procedural", using no special control codes, and computers not made by Fujitsu could also connect.

> [!note]
> 無手順 (*mutejun*, "no procedure") is the Japanese term for plain, unframed text transfer over a serial line, with no error-correcting file-transfer protocol. It meant any machine with a terminal program could use the service.

When NIFTY-Serve started, personal computers did not come with built-in modems. To connect to commercial PC communications such as NIFTY-Serve and PC-VAN, or to any of the other kinds of PC communications such as grassroots BBSes, you had to obtain separately an acoustic coupler, which fitted onto a telephone handset, or a modem connected to the computer's serial port. Early communication speeds were around 300 bps. For details, see the "System" section of the article on PC communications.

### Early years

At the start of the service there were 45 forums, and for the first [[#Forum managers and system operators|forum managers]] (system operators) NIFTY-Serve approached professionals in their fields and well-informed cultural figures.

Unlike ASCII-NET and PC-VAN, NIFTY-Serve charged from the very beginning, and because it offered services that put practical use ahead of hobbies (such as [[#Paid databases|paid databases]]), it had a large number of corporate members. Also, since fees could only be paid by credit card, membership was limited to people who had one.

For these reasons, in the 1980s PC communications were, if anything, more lively on PC-VAN. This can be called one of the factors that fixed NIFTY-Serve's culture, which was serious and was sometimes called stiff.

> [!note]
> PC-VAN later became part of BIGLOBE, which is how it appears in the December 2016 entry of [[The Internet - Nicopedia]].

### The arrival of the Internet era

In the first half of the 1990s NIFTY-Serve kept growing, with Fujitsu supporting the technology and Nissho Iwai the sales. On the system side it kept actively improving and extending its system, making it richer than competitors whose usability never changed. On the content side, its wide range of online software and its more active media-mix strategy than other companies raised its profile and drove its rapid growth. In 1995, riding the PC boom, its membership passed one million. It overtook the membership of PC-VAN, which had been ahead, and became the PC-communications service with the most users in Japan.

> [!note]
> This is the "million users around 1995" figure that [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] and [[4chan Pre-history]] give when they introduce NIFTY as the place where Ayashii World began.

However, the steady growth did not last. From around 1995 the "Internet", which could display a mix of media such as text and images, began to attract attention in Japan, and the NIFTY brand, known for PC communications, lost its advantage. Membership passed two million in 1996, [^5] but NIFTY's growth began to slow, and by 1997 it had almost levelled off.

Around this time NIFTY-Serve stopped growing for the first time since its launch. Some members who wanted more advanced communities left for more thriving places: the Internet, or the grassroots BBSes that had a second boom after the start of Telehodai. For example, "FQLD", a forum mainly for graphics data, had almost no activity left by 1996.

> [!note]
> *Telehodai* (テレホーダイ) was NTT's flat-rate telephone service for limited hours, started on August 22, 1995 (see [[#Fees]]). Grassroots BBSes charged nothing beyond the phone bill, so a flat-rate phone bill made them effectively free. Kowloon Kurosawa's grassroots BBS *Tokyo Isoternet*, which [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] dates to August 15, 1995, opened just as this was beginning.

Forums on specialist subjects and others that had no particular need for the Internet carried on as before. But as the members around them dwindled, NIFTY-Serve began to run short of information, and getting the latest information started to require using the Internet as well. By then, on hobby topics, the Internet was almost always ahead.

To respond to this, on October 1, 1997 NIFTY SERVE INTERWAY was launched, making it possible to reach NIFTY-Serve directly from the Internet without dialling up a NIFTY-Serve access point. [^6] And from April 1, 1998, a flat monthly fee was introduced for members who connected only via the Internet.

Meanwhile, the growing risks of speech in the community, such as the NIFTY lawsuit (the [[#NIFTY-Serve Contemporary Thought Forum case]]), made the parent companies Fujitsu and Nissho Iwai lose their active interest in PC communications.

### Decline after the merger with InfoWeb

On November 1, 1999, it merged with "InfoWeb", the Internet service provider run by its parent company Fujitsu. At the same time its corporate identity was changed to "**@nifty**", and the name of the word-processor and PC-communications service to "**NIFTY SERVE**". At this point Nissho Iwai withdrew from managing Nifty.

The merger was carried out with the Nifty side as the surviving company, but it was decided that the Internet connection service, which would be the main business from then on, would be provided by rebuilding the InfoWeb side's system. As a result @nifty's remote host name became infoweb.ne.jp rather than nifty.ne.jp. Because of this, the word-processor and PC-communications services, which depended on the old NIFTY-Serve membership system, could not be reached by users of the new system (@nifty members).

To deal with this, existing NIFTY-Serve members were not moved into the new system but were kept on as "old members". [^2] But this arrangement was preserved only for members who needed the word-processor and PC-communications services, and it forced inconveniences on NIFTY-Serve users, such as being unable to use some of the services offered to @nifty members. The shift to a passive way of running things, keeping the old NIFTY-Serve system as it was instead of renewing it, betrayed even the few users who tried to keep using NIFTY-Serve.

Around 2001, flat-rate plans such as Telehodai and FLET'S ISDN replaced dial-up PC communications as the norm, and many forums fell almost completely out of use. NIFTY scaled back how it presented NIFTY-Serve in advertising and the like, and began urging forums to use and move to the newly prepared "Forum@nifty" (Web forums). But the Web forums were clearly inferior to NIFTY-Serve's forum system, reducing a forum to a plain bulletin-board site centred on an administrator called a "sysop". Because of this, some forums gave up on moving to the Web forums and closed, and others stayed behind on PC communications. The old forum culture also did not suit the Web forums, which led to the fatal result that newcomers came to neither the new nor the old forums.

Eventually no new projects were started on NIFTY-Serve and it was only kept as it was, and most of its avant-garde sysops and members gave up on it and left. As a result there were no more ambitious forums. Most of those that remained were either places where the sysop and regulars just hung out together on the [[#Walls|"walls"]] and elsewhere, or ghost-town forums where the last post was months (sometimes years) old and anything you wrote got no reply from anyone. This atmosphere made it even harder for newcomers to join, and NIFTY-Serve declined.

### The end

Citing the fall in users, NIFTY ended the services it had provided as NIFTY-Serve in stages: communication services such as "[[#Forums|forums]]" and "[[#Bulletin boards|bulletin boards]]" on March 31, 2005; "[[#Patio (PATIO)|Patio]]" on May 31 of the same year; and all the remaining PC-communications services, including "[[#Interway|Interway]]", on March 31, 2006. This closed a history of about 19 years since the service began in 1987.

When the PC-communications service ended, the text-communication service over Telnet ended with it, and there was no small amount of criticism from users of dedicated word processors without a Web browser and from visually impaired users.

Some of the forums that existed until the service ended moved to "Forum@nifty" (the Web forums), but as with NIFTY-Serve they continued to see little use, and the Web forums themselves ended on March 31, 2007.

### Today

Nifty now concentrates on Internet services (for details see "@nifty"). Fujitsu sold its shares in Nifty to Nojima in 2017.

Nifty has made clear that it will not keep any record of NIFTY-Serve's past logs or the data in its data libraries, and apart from what staff and users have kept individually, these records are scattered.

Some forum users independently set up a new site, "folomy", which ran from April 1, 2007 to December 31, 2013; this service had nothing to do with Nifty. Many other forums also independently set up "forum successor sites" or "forum alumni sites". On the technical side, some built their own bulletin-board systems, some adopted forum software such as XOOPS or phpBB, some adopted social-network systems such as OpenPNE, and some took emergency refuge in blogs. Some services have also been revived on the Web, for limited periods.

- From April 15, 2011, a "NIFTY-Serve" experience corner was opened to mark Nifty's 25th anniversary. [^7]
- From May 24, 2012, a new "NIFTY-Serve" was run for a limited period of one year. [^8]

> The loss of the logs matters for the start of [[Ayashii World Hub|Ayashii World]]: [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] gives no archive link for Shiba's NIFTY Patio or Home Party, and dates them only by month or season.

## Overview

### Membership

To join NIFTY-Serve you had to buy a book that came with a membership right, called an introduction pack (*intro pack*), and sign up online, or else go through the paperwork by post (in later years intro packs were also bundled with retail PCs, modems and the like). Users had to handle by themselves the physical connection of the coupler or modem, the settings of the communications protocol, the modem's AT commands and so on, and these settings demanded a bare minimum of knowledge about communications. Unlike using today's PCs, which are sold with an Internet connection already set up, the hurdle to getting connected was high: people with no knowledge of network connections had no choice but to read the setup guide, so it also ended up teaching them a minimum of literacy.

### Connection

To connect to NIFTY-Serve over an ordinary telephone line, you went through Fujitsu's VAN network FENICS-ROAD. To connect, you had to choose the nearest of the FENICS-ROAD access points set up across Japan and dial up. In the early days some access points had only a few lines that could connect at once, for reasons such as population, and when they were hard to get through to, people would sometimes deliberately pay long-distance charges to connect to an access point in a city.

Access was also offered via TYMPAS, DDX-TP, VENUS-P, CompuServe, teleterminal communications, and the Telnet protocol on the Internet.

### Fees

NIFTY-Serve's charges were basically metered and depended on connection time. What is more, the telephone charges for reaching NIFTY-Serve were paid separately, and together with the bill from NTT they were a heavy burden for users (Telehodai, a flat-rate telephone service for limited hours, only began on August 22, 1995).

To save on these charges by shortening connection time, the development of autopilot programs flourished: the operations to perform while connected were entered in advance and handled by a macro. And because log browsers were developed to make the data obtained this way easier to read, users enjoyed a highly functional working environment, with unread-message management, post extraction and more.

| NIFTY-Serve network communication charge (FENICS-ROAD2, 2400 bps) | ¥10 per minute |

While its rival in PC communications, PC-VAN, had used a fixed monthly fee of ¥2,000 from the start, NIFTY-Serve was slow to follow. The reason is said to be that part of the communication charges was paid to the forum operators, and negotiating with the operators took time. NIFTY-Serve then introduced a discounted rate of ¥8 per minute from 3 a.m. to 8 a.m. Because the discount did not apply if you stayed connected, people chatting could be seen logging out once and back in. In 1999 a semi-flat rate of ¥5,000 for 50 hours was set; beyond 50 hours it was ¥6 per minute. The 50-hour limit was later abolished, and in the end the fee was revised to ¥2,000 a month, the same as PC-VAN, excluding chat.

> [!note]
> The note on grassroots BBSes in [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] says that NIFTY-Serve "charged users a monthly fee". By this article, in 1995, when Shiba was on NIFTY, the charge was metered by the minute, on top of the phone bill. The flat ¥2,000 monthly fee was PC-VAN's, and NIFTY only reached it much later.

### Connection account (ID)

To connect to NIFTY-Serve you needed an authentication number (account). As a rule one connection account was issued per person, in the form XXX99999: three letters followed by five digits. Later it became possible to obtain, for each connection account, a "child ID" of the form VFx99999, beginning with VF. However, all charges were paid by the parent ID, and some functions were restricted. At first the idea was that you could give an ID to family or friends as a present, but because of a string of fraud cases in the buy-and-sell corners, it was changed so that only users with the same surname could be registered (child-ID users with a different surname had their IDs confiscated and were made to take out a new parent ID). When NIFTY-Serve first began operating, you could tell from an ID "which intro pack it was registered with" (there were also kits for members-only CUGs and for users using it in place of a company intranet, and for kits bundled with PCs or modems, you could even tell which manufacturer's product it came with).

### Handles

Members did not have to reveal their real names. Each member chose a nickname called a handle, and members communicated with each other by handle. Handles could be set per forum, and people did all sorts of things: using different handles in different forums, joining several forums under a common handle, or deliberately using their real name as their handle. Although you could take part without revealing your real name, members' identities were known from the contract stage, so the operators could rein in members who behaved badly, and anonymous mischief did not mean much. However, because this was a time when awareness of security and personal-information protection was still low, theft, borrowing and impersonation of IDs and passwords sometimes became a problem, and, as described below, there was even a case in which the personal information of a member whose posts were seen as a problem was tracked down and became the subject of national news coverage.

> [!note]
> This is the point behind the note in [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] that "Pasokon-Tsushin required user ID, so that administrator could identify who posted easily". A handle on NIFTY was a pen name over a known, billed identity, which is the opposite of the nameless posting that Ayashii World and later [[2channel Hub|2channel]] made normal. See [[#Leak of members' personal information]] for the national news case.

### Profiles

A profile could be registered for an ID and viewed by entering a command such as PROF ID. It was mainly used as a self-introduction, setting out the member's interests, recent news and the like, and it was often consulted in [[#Chat|chat]] to find out who the people present were.

This is not limited to profiles, but at the time everything was written on the assumption that only NIFTY-Serve members would read it, so no thought was given to the possibility of it being read by outsiders, and there was no concept of protecting personal information. Together with copyright problems, this makes it hard to reuse the records of the time, such as past logs.

### Member information

Based on the contract information given when joining, every member's ID, real name, prefecture and terminal were made public and could be searched as member information. Later it became possible to make this private, but some services, such as "the Spirit (Kokoro) Corner", could not be used unless your member information was public.

### Typical commands

The lowercase part of a command could be omitted. NIFTY-Serve made no distinction between full-width and half-width characters in commands.

#### General commands

GO [GO command]

Jumps to the GO command assigned to each service. With this you could enter the service you wanted in one go without working down from the top menu, so it was the most used command.

PROF [ID]

Shows the profile of the specified ID.

E

Exits from the current location; quit.

OFF, BYE

Ends the connection.

/E

While entering text, ends input and moves on to confirmation. /EX and // do the same.

/POST

Basically the same as /E, but does not ask you to confirm the content or whether to send it.

#### Forum commands

SAY

Starts entering a post in a conference room.

COMPOSE

Starts entering a reply to the post you just read in a conference room.

RESP

Sends mail to the author of the post you just read in a conference room or on a bulletin board.

MREAD

Shows all unread posts in a conference room at once.

UST

Shows a list of the IDs of users currently in the same forum. There was also a command, UST2, that showed handles as well as IDs.

SEND [ID] [message]

Sends a one-line message to an ID currently in the same forum. The recipient saw it as a message interrupting whatever they were doing. If you did not want to receive these, you could refuse them with SEND OFF.

QUIT

If you had joined a forum, you could leave it with this command.

#### Chat commands

In chat, basically everything you type is treated as speech, so commands were prefixed with a slash (/).

/TAlk [job number]

Proposes a talk (private-conversation function) to the user with the specified job number. If the other person accepted, the two could talk in a place the other users could not see. For a conversation limited to three or more people, they agreed on a scramble code in advance and used the scramble function.

/Users [channel]

Shows a list of the participants currently connected to the channel.

/HAndle [handle]

Changes your handle in the chat.

/Channel [channel number]

Changes channel.

/Page [job number] [message]

Basically the same as SEND in forums: sends a one-line message to the participant with the specified job number. You could refuse these with /Nopage.

/SCramble [code]

Scramble function: your posts become visible only to others who have set the same code.

/SQuelch [ID]

Hides the posts of the specified ID. This is an ignore function, used when a troll appeared and so on.

/ID [ON,OFF]

Sets whether the poster's ID is shown alongside each post.

/E

Quit.

## Email

The connection account doubled as an email address: to exchange email within NIFTY-Serve, you just put the other person's ID (XXX99999) as the address. (At first mail could only be exchanged between members, but in 1992 it became possible to exchange mail with PC-VAN, and later that same year with Internet mail.) Between members, proof of delivery was available. Also, as long as the recipient had not yet received it, the sender could delete mail they had sent from the recipient's inbox. Other functions included date-specified mail, which arrived on the date you set when sending it, and an electronic voice mail service that let you receive mail by fax or hear it read out by machine over the telephone.

## Forums

A forum is a service in which members interested in a particular hobby or topic can communicate with each other. At first there were few forums, one forum per theme, but later, partly as company policy, more than 1,000 forums came into being as hobbies and interests became more finely divided. Forum names were written in half-width letters and numbers: the first letter was F, short for forum, followed by letters based on the theme. For example, there were forums such as FGAL (*efugaru*, *efugyaru*: GAL is short for gallery), dedicated to software, and FTERM (*efutāmu*), from "terminal", for communications.

If a topic took off and the number of participants grew, the increase in connection hours fed into the sysop's income. So sysops aimed to improve the service further by enriching the content, and by selecting the topics of each subdivided forum even more strictly, they could handle specialised topics. More participants also meant more trouble, but in many cases it proved effective for accumulating information. Also, although using a handle was normal, unlike on anonymous bulletin boards each post displayed the member ID, so individuals could be identified and the accuracy of information could be judged. Because of this, not a few former NIFTY-Serve members loathe today's anonymous bulletin boards.

Active forums sometimes set up several forums and divided them by purpose and content, and sometimes the subdivided forums were run independently (in some cases after rebelling).

### Forum features

A forum had the features "Notices", "Bulletin board", "Electronic conferences", "Data library", "Member information", "Real-time conferences (chat)" and "Mail to SYSOP", and the sysop could use them to provide services to the forum's members. For example, whether to use the chat feature was up to the sysop. In some forums the sysop took part in chat and enjoyed the conversation; in others they found it a bother and left it to the staff.

As for joining, there were forums that welcomed members without limit, forums where membership was approved after a waiting period of a few days, forums you could join with the approval of the sysop (or sub-sysop), and forums that admitted only people introduced by existing members.

#### Notices

The place where the sysop posted notices from the forum, mainly used to publish the forum's local rules. It was also sometimes used to announce maintenance or to post download rankings for the data library.

Other ways of informing members were the opening message shown when you opened the forum and the "three-times" message (shown under the opening message for your first three visits).

#### Bulletin board (forum)

A place where users (or only the administrators) could leave announcements and the like for the forum's members. Because the system did not allow comments, posts were hard to pin down, and posts disappeared after a few weeks, so it seems that most forums either did not use it at all or used it only for notices from the staff.

#### Electronic conferences

A forum could create up to 20 (10 in the early days) electronic conference rooms, called *conference rooms*, and the sysop (or staff) set the theme of each. For example, the sysop could divide them into a room for beginners, a room for advanced topics, a room just for chatting, a room for offline meet-ups and so on (from the members' point of view, if you thought chatting or meet-ups were unnecessary, you could just skip those rooms). Each conference room worked as a place for discussion and communication on its theme. The usual style was for the board leader or members versed in the theme to offer a topic and exchange opinions, but in lively rooms the members often got things going by themselves without the staff joining in.

The style of exchanging opinions in conference rooms was to attach comments (*res*, replies) to a post, possibly several. This is similar to the tree-type bulletin boards seen here and there on today's Internet. Some posts never got a comment, while others got many, with other people replying to replies addressed to someone else ("Sorry to cut in", "A side reply, but…"), until some grew into trees so bizarrely complex that it was hard to grasp the whole. Posts in a conference room could be deleted by their author or by the sysop. If the author wanted to withdraw a post, they deleted it themselves, but a post that had replies could not be deleted. When the sysop judged a post inappropriate, it could be deleted with sysop privileges, regardless of whether it had replies.

A single post could be up to 300 lines long, and a conference room could hold at most 999 or 65,000 posts (512 posts in the early days). If the same theme was to continue, the sysop had to create a new conference room. The most that could be viewed at once in a conference room was 999 posts (512 in the early days), and once that was exceeded no more could be written. In cyclic conference rooms, old posts were pushed out and deleted like *tokoroten* being squeezed through a press. In rooms with many posts, people sometimes competed over how fast the room cycled, or commented, compared with how fast it used to be, that it had "calmed down" or "gone quiet, hasn't it". The record that corresponds to the minutes of a conference room's conversation was called the "log". When a room hit its limit, it was usually stored in the data library described below, compressed or uncompressed. Keen members would download and diligently read past logs from before they joined, and sometimes advised lost beginners to read the past logs too (when being cold, people often just said "Read the past logs" and left it at that).

#### Data library

The data library served as a place to store text files, binary files and files in NAPLPS format. Users could register their own software, data and so on, using non-procedural transfer for text files and a binary transfer protocol (XMODEM or B Plus) for binary files. Registered files were made public once an administrator such as the sysop had checked in advance that they worked and checked them for viruses, and then approved them. Many online software titles, data collections, templates, dictionaries and so on were registered in the data libraries, and NIFTY-Serve members could download them freely (though they had to register as members of the forum).

Now that the service has ended, the data stored in these data libraries can no longer be accessed, but some of it has been registered on download sites such as Vector and can be obtained from the MS-DOS sections and so on.

#### Member information (forum)

You could get a list of the IDs and handles of all members who had joined the forum. Because collecting personal information from it became rampant, it was disabled in all forums in 1997.

#### Real-time conferences

Each forum had two (one in the early days) real-time conference rooms (RT) (some forums did not use them). A real-time conference is what is called chat: once you joined, you could communicate in real time, in text only, with the other members taking part, like today's IRC. In real-time conferences, to make up for the lack of information in text-only communication, many forms were used that developed further the emoticons (kaomoji) used until then. These kaomoji were passed on to the Internet and also influenced mobile-phone email and so on.

> [!note]
> [[Ayashii World|Ayashii World (Japanese Wikipedia)]] notes that Ayashii World made heavy use of particular kaomoji, such as ヽ(´ー｀)ノ. This article does not say where those came from.

There was also an independent chat service unconnected with any forum (CB), with almost the same features as the chat inside forums.

#### Mail to SYSOP

A shortcut for sending mail to the sysop: with it you could get in touch immediately without looking up the sysop's ID (though the sysop could set which ID it was sent to, and it was sometimes forwarded to forum staff). Under their contract with Nifty, sysops were obliged to support users, so this served as the contact point for user enquiries. However, almost all forums encouraged people to ask about general topics, the kind handled in the conference rooms, in the conference rooms, and accepted only questions and requests mainly about running the forum.

### Running a forum

#### Proposing a forum

You planned a forum, wrote it up as a proposal and submitted it to Nifty. If it was accepted, you became a forum manager and could open the forum.

There was a principle of **one theme, one forum**: a forum on a theme that overlapped with an existing forum was not approved (even if the existing forum was inadequate). However, existing forums were not prevented from covering themes handled by other specialist forums, so as a result there were many cases of several forums covering similar themes (for example, MIDI topics were covered not only in the dedicated forum FMIDI but also in software forums such as FGALAV, FWINMM and FGALRAY).

#### Forum managers and system operators

On NIFTY-Serve, running a forum was entrusted to the **forum manager** who planned it. There was also a role called **system operator** (sysop, SYSOP), who did the actual management and maintenance work, but as a rule the forum manager held this role as well. However, some forums (those that had grown large, and especially sub-forums) appointed a separate sysop.

Forum managers and system operators were in a contractual relationship with Nifty. The forum manager bore responsibility under the contract for running the forum that had been set up, and the system operator could set local rules for the forum, which members were obliged by the membership terms to follow.

Basically, forum managers were given IDs beginning with SDI, and system operators who were not forum managers IDs beginning with STF. These IDs had no billing address, so they could use NIFTY-Serve for the cost of the phone call alone.

Most sysops were amateurs, enthusiasts of their fields, but sometimes professionals (experts) took the role. The ideal sysop had knowledge and insight in the field, could liven up discussion, could mediate when trouble arose, and was well respected. For each forum, part of the total connection charges of its members was paid to the sysop as remuneration. This was called the kickback, and some people earned more than ¥40 million a year as a sysop. [^3] Sysops' income was kept secret in the early days, but later, depending on the situation, it came to be revealed that remuneration existed.

#### Sub-system operators and board leaders

Sub-system operator (SUB-SYSOP, *sabushisu*, or forum staff)

Also called a non-contract system operator. They were given the same powers as a system operator, or some of them. As forum staff assisting the system operator, for example, a staff member in charge of the [[#Data library|data library]] was given the power to use the data library's maintenance commands.

Board leader (BL, chair)

The moderator or organiser of a particular [[#Electronic conferences|electronic conference room]]. They had no special powers.

Basically the system operator could appoint either of these at their own discretion, and they were regarded as ordinary members cooperating in running the forum. A setting called the free flag was turned on for these members' IDs, which exempted them from communication charges in that forum.

#### Sysop powers

Sysops could manage the communication logs and, it was said, could (if they felt like it) see information such as which member entered which conference room and when. They also had the power to expel members and delete posts. While a friendly discussion was going on, nobody was particularly aware of this, but when trouble did occur, these sysop powers counted. Members who were forcibly expelled, of course, tended to bear a deep grudge. Now and then you would see such a member get another ID, secretly rejoin, and relentlessly repeat criticism of the sysop and staff.

As for appointing sysops, although there was a condition of "having some track record", it existed in name only, and there was in practice no check on whether the person was suitable to represent a forum, a body with hundreds to thousands of members. So while forums whose sysop had a good sense of balance were fine, forums whose sysop had a problematic personality saw trouble break out often as membership grew, and in some cases the scars remain to this day. Forums whose names became known even outside Nifty because of the many troubles caused by their sysops included the Movie Forum, the NEC Forum, the Railway Forum and the Defense Review (military enthusiasts) Forum.

### Rise and fall of forums

Because each forum gathered people versed in its topic, discussion among like-minded people took off. But when many people gather, the chance of trouble such as quarrels also rises. When trouble occurred, staff such as the sysop would step in to mediate or put it down using their powers, but depending on the forum various rules were developed (forum rules): directing beginners to the past logs and to the right conference room, dealing with problem posts such as slander, self-restraint about copyright such as quoting song lyrics or books, and so on. The ones common to many forums became established as etiquette on the net, "netiquette". For people who lacked the courage to post for the first time and could not bring themselves to post, books that collected and explained this netiquette were published, such as *PC Communications "Unspoken Understandings" Encyclopedia: 125 Pieces of Wisdom for Not Being Daunted by Its Peculiar Atmosphere* (by Omimi, Media Tech Publishing, 1995, ISBN 4-944080-23-9). Many of these rules are still useful today on bulletin boards, blogs and elsewhere, even though the environment has changed greatly.

### Stations

A station is a forum planned and run by a third-party company. Functionally it was the same as a forum, but it was set up at the company's request, and the company's public-relations staff or similar acted as station manager and handled its management. It worked as an official community before the Internet became widespread, for user support, announcements to users and so on.

## Bulletin boards

Separately from the forums and conference rooms, bulletin boards were provided for exchanging information and socialising between members.

On NIFTY-Serve, registering a post on a bulletin board, and the post itself, was called a **posting** (掲示, *keiji*, literally sticking it up on the board). When posting on a bulletin board, you could set **search keys** in advance for filtering. Based on these, readers could read only the kinds of postings they wanted. Each posting also had a counter showing how many times it had been read. Up to 512 postings were kept; they were deleted when their retention period ran out or, once there were more than 512, from the oldest.

Unlike in the [[#Electronic conferences|conference rooms]], the bulletin boards had no serial numbers for posts, so a post could not be uniquely identified, which got in the way of organising logs.

On the Spirit (Kokoro) Corner and the buying and selling boards, the real name taken from the member's joining information was automatically inserted into each posting.

The list of bulletin boards as of 1994 was as follows.

1. How to use
2. Looking for companions
3. Pen-pal corner
4. Giving away / Please let me have
5. For sale
6. Wanted to buy
7. Please tell me / I'll tell you
8. [[#Spirit (Kokoro) Corner (BBS 8)|Spirit (Kokoro) Corner]]
9. Hometown board

## Chat

### CB Simulator

Modelled on CompuServe's CB Simulator, this reproduced citizens band radio on a text terminal. There were Band A and Band B (Band C was added later), each band had channels 1 to 30, and people connected to the same channel could talk in real time. Of these, channel 2 was the channel for beginners.

### Forum RTC (RT)

One of the forum features, with almost the same functions as the CB Simulator. For details see [[#Real-time conferences]].

### Chat rooms

An extension of the CB Simulator in which whoever opened a chat room became its administrator and could control the participants.

## Personal conference rooms

Bulletin boards and conference rooms that could be opened, one per ID. A fee was needed to open one. The owner could set the theme and so on freely, and there were all kinds: some invited the general public by setting no password or publishing it, and others were private and enjoyed among insiders.

### Home Party (HP)

A simple bulletin board where posts piled up one after another. Handles were not shown; only the ID, title, body and date and time of posting were displayed. Because it was easy to set up and manage, it was something like a message board.

A single post was limited to 20 lines. Also, the number of entries a Home Party kept was based not on the number of posts but on the total number of lines of all posts, and anything beyond 200 lines was deleted. Because of this, to save even a few lines, some Home Parties used symbols such as ◆ or ▼ in the text as so-called pseudo line-break marks, treating them as line breaks (the same technique as newspaper columns. The line limit and the number of lines kept could be extended to 100 lines/1,000 lines for an extra fee).

Because there was no field for a handle, a common convention was to write "/handle" after the title so that posters were easier to tell apart.

> [!note]
> This is the service on which Shiba ran the first *Ayashii World* in the summer–autumn of 1995. [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] describes it as "a simple anonymous BBS service", but by this article a Home Party post showed the poster's member ID, and every ID belonged to a known, billed member (see [[#Handles]]). It was handle-less, not anonymous. Part 1's own note ("UserID, Title, and 20 line sentence limits") agrees with this section. [[2ch History Timeline]] and [[4chan Pre-history]] describe the Home Party as a hosted website from an Internet provider. It was in fact this personal board inside NIFTY-Serve's own closed service, which a member rented for a fee.

### Patio (PATIO)

A repurposing of the forum's conference-room feature, which in effect let you have one conference room equivalent to a forum's. It had more management features than a Home Party: access control was possible, such as banning access from particular IDs or letting only users approved by the administrator use it.

> [!note]
> [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] dates Shiba's first NIFTY venture to April 1995: a music Patio named *musicaholic*, covering UK rock and techno. That is, he rented one of these private conference rooms before he opened the Home Party.

## Other services

Besides information exchange, NIFTY-Serve offered services of its own.

- A shareware remittance service, which handled remitting payments to shareware authors and issuing registration keys on their behalf
- A clipping service (Scrapbook), which notified you of headlines from newspaper articles once you had specified particular keywords in advance
- Since search engines like today's did not exist, an information service for books and so on by search professionals called *searchers*
- An online shopping mall
- Conversational services (chatbots) in which you could talk in text with the fictional characters "Kaiwa-kun" and "Oshaberi Kirara" (Kirara Ōmori)
- Games such as *Daihinmin*

### Paid databases

As its main attraction other than the forums, NIFTY-Serve offered more than 1,000 practical databases of every kind, from searches of past newspaper articles to flower market prices, supplied by information companies.

Use of these services fell as the Internet spread, but some people valued them because reliable information that could not easily be found on the Internet was available at once, because, like the other services, they could be used from word-processor communications, and because results came back as plain text with no decoration at all.

### NIFTY-Serve ES

A service for English speakers living in Japan, the English version of NIFTY-Serve. [^4] Only the mailbox was shared with the normal service; the forums and everything else were separate. There were forums such as an Amiga forum and a forum for English-conversation teachers.

### Services for businesses

Business accounts

A group membership system used, for example, to enrol the people of a company or organisation all at once.

Names and other details were self-declared, and the checks were looser than for ordinary members, so they were sometimes abused on some forums and bulletin boards for sock-puppetry and for manufacturing majorities, which was seen as a problem.

CUG (Closed Users Group)

A closed network using NIFTY-Serve's access points and system. You connected by the same procedure as NIFTY-Serve, and logging in with a CUG ID connected you to a dedicated network. Forums and bulletin boards were set up completely separately from NIFTY-Serve, and organisations and companies used it as an internal network.

Private forums (PF, Private Forum)

Non-public forums on NIFTY-Serve, which, like CUGs, organisations (such as the correspondence courses of vocational schools) and companies used as internal networks. Ordinary members could reach one if they knew its GO command, but to get inside they needed the approval of the sysop who managed it. Revealing the GO command of a non-public forum on the ordinary services was forbidden.

## Newsletter

NIFTY published a newsletter, "**OLTJ**" (ONLINE TODAY JAPAN), which carried information about NIFTY-Serve's forums and serialised columns by celebrities who used NIFTY-Serve. It was first published with the February 1987 issue. [^10] It was originally a paid monthly magazine, [^11] but it was sent free to NIFTY-Serve members (later only to those who had set the Executive Option). [^12] From the September–October 1997 issue [^13] it was renamed "**NIFTY SERVE MAGAZINE**". [^12]

After that, free distribution to members ended, [^14] and the title changed to "**Nifty super internet**" from the May 1999 issue [^15] and to "**walk@nifty**" from the April 2003 issue, [^16] with Internet information and the like at its centre, but it ceased publication with the August 2006 issue. [^16]

## Connecting to the Internet

NIFTY-Serve achieved interconnection with the Internet when, on September 1, 1992, it opened an email interconnection gateway together with the WIDE Project. When sending email from the Internet to NIFTY-Serve, niftyserve.or.jp was used as the domain name, but this later became nifty.ne.jp (with a change of settings, nifty.com can also be used).

It was said that if Internet websites were compared to individual shops, NIFTY-Serve was the equivalent of a department store or a shopping arcade of specialist shops that gathered a wide range of information in one place.

### WIDE Internet interconnection experiment

In 1992, an interconnection experiment with the WIDE Project made it possible to exchange email with the networks taking part in the WIDE Project. At this stage it could only connect to the address space managed by the Japan Network Information Center.

> [!note]
> The WIDE Project built the IP research network that succeeded JUNET; see the note on it in [[Internet Old-Timers' Club - My First JUNET]], which also dates the commercial ("proper") Internet in Japan to 1992.

### Internet connection

In 1994, connection with the Internet overseas made the following services available.

- Sending and receiving mail with the Internet
- A gateway for logging in to Telnet servers on the Internet and transferring files from FTP servers via NIFTY-Serve
- A service for reading netnews such as fj

> [!note]
> fj was the JUNET-era Japanese netnews hierarchy. See the note on it in [[Internet Old-Timers' Club - My First JUNET]].

### Telnet connection

From February 1, 1994, it became possible to connect to NIFTY-Serve from the Internet using the Telnet protocol. But although it had the advantage of costing nothing in phone charges, usage fees at the time were metered, so you could not stay connected as long as you liked, and it did little to increase use. Still, it had a loyal following, because email could be checked quickly and binary data could be exchanged fast using HyperTerminal, a standard feature of Windows 95 to 98.

### NIFTY Manager

An integrated GUI communications program dedicated to NIFTY-Serve, provided from 1995 for Windows and Macintosh. It could reach NIFTY-Serve by dial-up to a dedicated access point or by connecting over the Internet. Its settings and operation were simpler than using a text terminal, and light users liked it. As an integrated program it included an email client, but unlike modern email clients it did not support multiple accounts.

It was often bundled with PCs during the PC boom brought on by the arrival of Windows 95, and it contributed to the growth in membership.

### Interway

In July 1997, Interway (INTERWAY) was provided as a way of accessing the service from a Web browser. It carried the structure of the tty straight over onto HTTP, and it was not very easy to use.

> [!note]
> The [[#The arrival of the Internet era|History]] section above dates the launch of NIFTY SERVE INTERWAY to October 1, 1997, while this section says July 1997. The source cited there [^6] (September 25, 1997) is headlined as an *expansion* of Interway. The article does not reconcile the two dates.

### NNTP connection

From 1998, a service began that let you read and write in forum conference rooms from the Internet using the NNTP protocol. It could only read and write posts; operations such as deleting, joining and leaving had to be done by logging in another way.

### Palette

Web conference rooms for forums, set up from April 1, 1999. They were tree-style bulletin boards inside an authenticated area on a Web server, where you could use HTML decoration tags and attach images. But they were hardly used, because although they were inside the forum they could not be reached at all from PC communications, because the system linked to them only from inside Interway, and because the frame-heavy design made the screen cramped.

### SpiSee

A search site set up on the Web on July 1, 1999, which allowed full-text search of forums' [[#Electronic conferences|electronic conference rooms]] and [[#Palette|Palette]].

## Culture

### Vocabulary

#### On-gaki and off-gaki

These words were not limited to NIFTY-Serve, but especially in the early days, to save on [[#Fees|connection charges]], people would write their posts, replies, mail and so on in advance before connecting, and only send them while connected. This was called **off-gaki** ("off-writing", the *off* of offline). With off-gaki it was normal to take time revising, as with a letter or a manuscript, so that the text was easy to read with the reader in mind, and so that it used as few characters as possible (if a text was hard to understand, so that the same topic was repeated or new explanations had to be added, the burden of connection charges grew not only for the people in the discussion but for every other reader too. A discussion dragging on was itself sometimes seen as a problem and criticised). **On-gaki** is the opposite of off-gaki: typing text while connected (online) and sending it as is. Because it tended to produce hasty writing, it was sometimes frowned on.

#### Mente-ochi

When it began, NIFTY-Serve was open from 8 a.m. to 3 a.m. the next morning. If you stayed connected past this, or when the maintenance period began, the centre cut your connection automatically. This was called **mente-ochi** ("dropped for maintenance").

The disconnections were not simultaneous: depending on the connection and other conditions, the moment of mente-ochi varied by anything from tens of seconds to several minutes. Perhaps because of this, in NIFTY-Serve's early days (the 1980s) participants in a [[#Real-time conferences|forum RTC]] sometimes competed over who would stay on longest at mente-ochi.

It became a 24-hour service in April 1990.

#### Walls

In a forum's list of conference rooms, a line such as **====================** was sometimes put between conference rooms as a divider (for example, in the early History Forum it was used between the rooms for Japanese-history topics, the rooms for world history, and the special rooms). In reality these were just empty conference rooms given such a title. They were not supposed to be writable, but some had been left writable through configuration mistakes and the like.

Members who discovered this went from one such room to another and started chatting about things unrelated to the forum's theme, and divider rooms that could be used for this kind of chat came to be called *walls* (壁, *kabe*).

Later, dividers were sometimes deliberately made writable to liven up a forum, and by extension, conference rooms for free talk were also sometimes called walls.

### Forums

#### Auto Racing Information Forum (FMOTOR4)

Its selling point was F1 news flashes. Until the Internet spread, apart from watching the live broadcast or connecting to CompuServe in the sport's home countries, it was the fastest source of information. Forum staff sometimes even went to the Grand Prix itself and gave live commentary in the conference rooms. It is also famous because the manga artist Mitsuru Sugaya was involved in running it (Sugaya also served as the forum's sysop in its early days).

#### MIDI Forum (FMIDI)

Covered topics such as computer music, synthesizers and keyboards. Its data library carried many MIDI files of songs transcribed by ear and of original songs.

#### Natural Environment Forum (FENV)

A forum about the environmental-protection movement. Statements from environmental groups were posted, and environmental activists exchanged information.

#### Journal Online Network (FJON)

A virtual, non-sectioned journal that treated members' contributions as articles, with users contributing articles as reporters and commentators.

#### Contemporary Thought Forum (FSHISO)

A forum that grew in 1990 out of a group of bulletin boards that had been opened as [[#Home Party (HP)|Home Parties]]. Its theme was "contemporary society and thought". It was a forum where members presented their own opinions on these and debated with other members.

#### Software Gallery Forum (FGAL)

A gallery for online software, carrying programs and data for many machine types, whether freeware, shareware or otherwise. It was NIFTY-Serve's flagship forum and the largest in scale.

It held such a wide range of programs and data that, before the Internet spread, the cover disks of computer magazines carried almost nothing but programs reprinted from it.

#### Windows Forum (FWINDOWS)

A forum set up with the cooperation of the industry body the Windows Consortium. Established in the Windows 3.1 era, it rapidly increased its participants and registered software after Windows 95 went on sale, and later became a forum group with the largest number of forums under it. Hidemaru Editor, famous as shareware, and the communications program NIFterm also had their support venues here.

#### QLD Forum (FQLD)

NIFTY-Serve's version of PC-VAN's QLD image communications, covering everything from the development of graphics tools to the publication of image data.

#### National BBS Forum (FBBS)

A forum about PC communications, which gathered exchanges of information about host programs, operating information on grassroots BBSes, directories of their phone numbers and so on.

#### Sharp Products Users' Forum (FSHARP)

A forum for users of Sharp's MZ-series and X-series personal computers, pocket computers and so on. At its height there was a period when it was split into several forums. For an outline of the legalisation of non-commercial emulators, see the section "[[#Things that started on NIFTY-Serve]]".

#### Architecture Forum (FARCHI)

A forum on the theme of architecture, organised as three forums (the CAD Hall, the Design Hall and the High-Tech Hall). Jw_cad was also born and developed in this forum.

#### Japanese Language Forum (FNIHONGO)

A private forum opened by Ten Takezoe of Gobun Kenkyūsha with his own money. Besides general questions about the Japanese language, it also had conference rooms on teaching Japanese to foreigners.

### Bulletin boards

#### Free Talk (BFREE)

An electronic bulletin board similar in character to PC-VAN's Free Board and to today's anonymous bulletin boards, but because it tended to turn into battles, unlike PC-VAN's it was later made usable only by members whose personal information was set to public. Meanwhile, people who could not get used to this kind of atmosphere disappeared, before anyone noticed, from BFREE and in turn from NIFTY-Serve.

#### Spirit (Kokoro) Corner (BBS 8)

A bulletin board set up as a successor after BFREE ended, a place to write freely about one's thoughts and opinions. But battles between participants often broke out over political claims, religious promotion and the like, and some members enjoyed watching them.

### Communities

#### Reikon ch

→ For details see "Reikon ch"

An anonymous chat held regularly every Friday, from about 1995 to 1997, on channel 8 of Band A of the [[#CB Simulator]]. It was given this name because all its participants, as a rule, called themselves **Reikon** (霊魂, "spirit"). Even after it lost its anonymity with the addition of the ID command in October 1997, chats in which everyone's handle was "Reikon" were held frequently until around the end of 1999.

> [!note]
> In 1995–1997 this ran at the same time as Shiba's Home Party and the move of [[Ayashii World Hub|Ayashii World]] onto the Internet. A shared default handle is the same idea as the later "nameless" (名無し) posting of [[Amezou]] and [[2channel Hub|2channel]]. Even so, the operators could still identify everyone here (see [[#Handles]]). The "ID command" is presumably the /ID [ON,OFF] chat command (see [[#Chat commands]]), which shows each speaker's ID next to their posts.

#### Ayashii World

"Ayashii World" (あやしいわーるど), which became the forerunner of anonymous bulletin boards on the Internet, initially (1995) used NIFTY-Serve's [[#Home Party (HP)|Home Party]]. For what happened after that, see [[Ayashii World Hub|Ayashii World]].

> [!note]
> The original spells the heading あやしいワールド (with *wārudo* in katakana), the spelling [[History of Ayashii World - Part 1]] gives for the 1995 Home Party, and spells it あやしいわーるど (in hiragana) in the text, the name of the later Internet boards. Part 1 places the Home Party in the summer–autumn of 1995, after Shiba's *musicaholic* Patio of April 1995, and says it "closes soon afterwards". The Japanese Wikipedia article [[Ayashii World|Ayashii World]] likewise says the community "initially used Home Party on NIFTY-Serve" before moving to Digital Eden and Kenjinkai. The [[Ayashii World Hub]] timeline starts at 1996-04 and leaves out this NIFTY period.

#### Battle Watcher Patio (BWP)

A Patio, set up from 1998, for watching the disputes that broke out on NIFTY-Serve's bulletin boards and forums. "The Spirit (Kokoro) Corner" and "the Contemporary Thought Forum" were the main things it watched.

### Volunteering

During the Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake, information was actively exchanged on NIFTY-Serve from the day of the earthquake: reports on the damage, checks on people's safety, requests for relief supplies and so on. On January 26, 1995, the **Earthquake Volunteer Forum** (**FSHINSAI**) was set up to gather this information, and with its activities reported in the news, PC communications attracted the spotlight in the recovery.

This forum later developed into **Inter V Net**, a shared conference room in which major PC-communications services such as PC-VAN and ASCII-NET worked together with The Network News, a newsgroup run by IIJ, but it wound down without ever being put to use.

### Theme song

There is a theme song, "**Kanashimi no NIFTY-Serve**" ("Sad NIFTY-Serve", sung by syaski, words and music by Yowochin), by a band called "Back-uppers" formed by volunteers from the FILLY & Multimedia Forum (FFILLY). It was also included on the CD supplement to the August 1995 issue of the newsletter "OLTJ", making it a semi-official Nifty theme song, and it was sung at events such as the 1995–96 "NETWORKERS JAPAN Offline Festival" and, when NIFTY-Serve ended in 2006, "Goodbye, PC Communications".

### Things that started on NIFTY-Serve

- The place where LHA's source code was published, and its primary distribution point, was the Software Workshop (FLABO).
- The drafting software JW_CAD was made by a member of the Architecture Forum (FARCHI), and spread after being registered in its data library.
- The Integrated Archiver Project started in FWINDEV (later FWINDB).
- The ROM images and system disks (such as Human68k) of Sharp's personal workstation X68000 were released free of charge as a result of lobbying in the SHARP Products Users' Forum (FSHARP).
- The term "light novel" originated in SF Fantasy (FSF).

## Trouble

### NIFTY-Serve Contemporary Thought Forum case

The so-called NIFTY lawsuit drew attention as the first trial in Japan to ask whether posts on the net could constitute defamation, and whether a network administrator bears a managerial responsibility to delete posts (it is also called the "NIFTY-Serve Contemporary Thought Forum case" to distinguish it from other lawsuits later brought in connection with NIFTY-Serve). It was filed in 1994; the first-instance judgment was handed down on May 27, 1997, and the second-instance judgment on September 5, 2001. It began in the "Feminism Conference Room" [^17] set up in the Contemporary Thought Forum (FSHISO, *efushiso*). Judging from the forum's name, it might seem to deal with lofty topics, but in reality people of different thought, beliefs and religion engaged in fierce debate, and scenes of mutual abuse were often seen.

> [!note]
> The first-instance date here (May 27, 1997) does not match the article's own source [^17], the court PDF, which gives the original judgment date as Heisei 9 (1997) May 26.

The outline of the case is as follows. A newly joined male member held critical views of a female member, who was also on the forum's staff, and repeatedly posted about her in a style that could be called provocative. For her part, the female member pried into the man's privacy, such as his workplace, and excluded him from chats in the real-time conference room (RT). The man kept up the provocation and, about her two experiences of abortion and miscarriage, which she had mentioned in a personal memoir about her divorce that she had contributed herself, denounced her as a "fetus killer" and "infant killer". The man's denunciations escalated further, reaching the level of slander. However, on the subject of the abortions, other members also directed critical opinions at the female member. In the course of this, the female member almost stopped posting in the forum. Meanwhile, in mail to the sysop, she demanded that the man's posts be deleted, on condition that her request be kept secret. The sysop's policy was: "Since democratic discussion within the forum should proceed under the principle of self-responsibility, as a rule I do not delete posts on the sysop's own judgment, or while concealing that the person concerned has requested it." So the sysop urged discussion between the parties and open debate within the forum. Dissatisfied with this, the woman brought a civil suit to pursue the man's tort liability for defamation, and the sysop's and Nifty's liability for breach of contract through breach of a duty to delete (breach of a duty of supervision). The first-instance judgment ordered all three defendants to pay damages, including on the supervisory liability of the sysop and Nifty. The second-instance judgment found that, given the nature of the forum, the sysop's operating policy had been reasonable, and denied the liability of the sysop and Nifty (though the man's defamation was upheld and became final). The male member had also filed a counterclaim against the female member, arguing that excluding him (scrambling) from the RT conference room amounted to so-called *mura hachibu* (ostracism) and was a tort (defamation). On the counterclaim, the court, while assessing the female member's scrambling as "conduct suggesting a lack of basic understanding of the need for tolerance of opposing opinions" (second-instance judgment), dismissed the counterclaim on the ground that she had only excluded him temporarily during a single chat, so that legally it could not be found to be unlawful to a degree comparable to *mura hachibu*. [^18]

> [!note]
> "Scrambling" him means using the /SCramble chat command (see [[#Chat commands]]) so that the man could no longer see the conversation. *Mura hachibu* (村八分) is the traditional village punishment of collective ostracism.

### NIFTY-Serve Books and Magazines Forum case

A case over a "dispute" that took place in a conference room and a Patio of NIFTY-Serve's Books and Magazines Forum. It drew attention as a judgment in which the court recognised the doctrine of counter-speech.

According to the plaintiff X, a member, another member A had repeatedly made unlawful statements against X amounting to insult or defamation, but Nifty had left these statements alone, tacitly approving an unlawful state of affairs, and despite X's requests had not disclosed A's name and address. X sued Nifty for damages and for disclosure of A's name and address. Against this claim, the defendant Nifty argued that one should not single out part of the statements in a conference room and judge them insulting or defamatory; when the exchange as a whole and its flow are viewed, including the other party's rebuttals, if it is merely a "quarrel", it is neither defamation nor insult and is not a tort. It contested the case in full.

The court, while accepting that A's statements had been insulting to X, denied that they were unlawful on the grounds, among others, that X's rebuttals had had the effect of preventing a lowering of X's social standing (the doctrine of counter-speech), and dismissed all of X's claims. [^19] [^20]

Commentary on this case appears in works such as *Media Hanrei Hyakusen* (100 Selected Media Cases; Yuhikaku, a *Jurist* supplement).

### The "M-chan" incident

On August 14, 1994, a posting appeared on the [[#Bulletin boards|bulletin board]] "Please tell me / I'll tell you". It was a string of obscene words soliciting mail from men, posted by someone else who had illicitly obtained the password of an ID. Because the ID was registered in a woman's name, some members took it at face value, responded, and sent mail. The same day, the mail was posted by the same ID, and the senders were pilloried along with their real names. The next day, some of the exposed members noticed and posted a mass of dummy postings, pushing the offending posts off the board and erasing them, but by then the log had already spread to other networks. This was before the Act on the Prohibition of Unauthorized Computer Access (the Unauthorized Access Prohibition Act) took effect, and the culprit has not been identified. The incident drew attention, being reported in the newspapers among other things. Several copycat incidents also followed.

> [!note]
> Pushing a post off the board by flooding it works here because a bulletin board kept only the 512 newest postings (see [[#Bulletin boards]]). The real names appeared because the boards attached members' real names and IDs (see [[#Member information]]).

### Leak of members' personal information

Around 1996, on one of the forums, the personal information of a member who had allegedly repeatedly posted in breach of the net's rules and manners, such as that member's workplace, was tracked down and reported nationally. At the time, although awareness of the importance of protecting personal information was general, the Personal Information Protection Act had not yet taken effect, and the forum's sysop repeatedly spoke publicly, in the media and on the net, about how the personal information had been tracked down and so on. The name of the forum and the sources are omitted.

### Toshiba complainer incident

→ For details see "Toshiba complainer incident"

Also known as "NIFTY-Serve's last incident". A series of uproars stirred up by Akky, a company employee in Fukuoka who had bought a mass-market video recorder at an electronics store, over Toshiba's handling of his complaint. The case is taken up in many places as an example of how companies and consumers relate in the Internet age, but it began on NIFTY-Serve.

Akky, who called himself an enthusiast of cutting-edge AV equipment, took part in NIFTY-Serve's Audio Visual Forum (FAV), gathering information and exchanging opinions actively. He wrote about the purchase at Best Denki that started it all in a conference room the same day. From then on he kept a record of every exchange with the store, the repair centre and Toshiba by writing it up in the conference room. However, A's conduct led to conflict with the conference room's regular participants, and in the end the system operator deleted his posts.

> [!note]
> The original switches here from "Akky" to "A", apparently a slip; both refer to the same man.

This deletion was the starting point: Akky came into conflict with FAV's management, stopped posting in the conference room, and built his own site on "member.nifty.ne.jp", the homepage service for NIFTY-Serve members that had begun only the year before (members could use 2 MB of space for free). He posted the past logs as LZH-compressed text and, at the same time, published the audio file that could be called the core of the incident. As a result, a boycott of Toshiba products spread on anonymous bulletin boards such as [[2channel Hub|2channel]].

> [!note]
> This fills in the start of the story told in [[2channel (2ch.net)]], where the incident is the reason 2channel's traffic took off in 1999, and in [[2004 - Japanese Find a Forum to Vent Most-Secret Feelings]], which says the recording was uploaded onto "Channel 2". By this article the recording was published on Akky's own NIFTY member homepage, and 2channel was where the boycott spread.

### Fraud

A case that was widely reported and drew public attention was a series of frauds committed from April 1996 by the same culprit, who claimed to be selling PC CPUs, memory and so on. It was a typical fraud: using so-called insider information, the culprit opened fictitious bank accounts, posed as a third party, and swindled cash out of buyers who paid into these accounts. On May 9, 1997, the defendant, a company employee, was found guilty in Courtroom 111 of the Kyoto District Court and sentenced to two years in prison, suspended for three years (the NIFTY-Serve electronic bulletin board fraud case, Kyoto District Court, Heisei 8 (wa) Nos. 1226 and 1312 and Heisei 9 (wa), published in *Hanrei Jihō* No. 1613, p. 156).
