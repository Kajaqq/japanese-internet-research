**amezo.com does not bring you here.**

## Overview

On August 5, 1997, Amezou created an underground-oriented link directory called “Amezou Link.” Before even Google existed, reaching underground sites required obtaining their URLs somewhere; link directories saved users that trouble. However, sites hosting illegally uploaded “warez” and “MP3s” would be forced to close when traffic concentrated on them. Above all, hordes of “chūbō” would arrive. In that sense, link directories broke an unwritten rule.[^tn-ame-chubo]

> You are free to link to Amezou Link. Please link to it freely, anywhere. (Also, if you email me or post on the board to register a link with Amezou Link, I intend to link to everything.) I've changed my mind; I really can't do that. I'll give priority to people who link back.
>
> *Text at the bottom of the Amezou Link page, probably around 1998.*

Some underground users therefore disliked Amezou Link. Eventually, however, link directories proliferated as a genre, and Amezou Link came to be recognized as a pioneer. Amezou updated the directory frequently and was gentle and courteous when appearing on the board, so underground users gradually grew fond of him too. Shiba, administrator of Ayashii World, then the largest underground message-board community, also appears to have been on friendly terms with him. There is testimony that Shiba disliked him, but no logs specifically demonstrating that survive; it probably refers to the period immediately after Amezou Link began.

> Poster: [blank] Posted: Monday, 2000/04/24, 01:12:11 ■ ★
>
> Amezou was originally a participant on Ayashii, and originally set up underground sites for tools, warez, and so on.
>
> I don't know much about Amezou back then either, but apparently he did something that made an enemy of Shiba, and Ayashii was quite hostile toward him.
>
> There was even a prankster who hacked some site, impersonated Amezou, and changed its front page to something like “Amezou's Holiday?”
>
> Then he launched a message-board site in what is now the 2channel format, and Amezou itself got to the point of being featured as a site of the week or something on a mainstream news site like PC Watch.
>
> I suppose it gradually lost its underground character.
>
> Hiroyuki imitated that and succeeded. He himself says it isn't an underground site.
>
> Everything Hiroyuki does is so chūbō-like that it's hard to believe he's founded a company, but his openness is unparalleled.

Amezou Link was primarily a directory of message boards. It also had its own boards, originally for posting new links and changed board addresses. There were boards such as News Flash and Stock News Flash; among the names appearing there was Kirikomi Taichō, who would later become famous on 2ch.

Ayashii World closed on September 3, 1998. Its sudden closure shocked users: so many people had been glued to it that each refresh brought an entire screenful of new posts.

On September 6, 1998, Amezou improved the ordinary message-board software he had installed himself, converting it into a thread-floating board. Its ease of use made Amezou enormously popular almost overnight.

No successor to Ayashii World had specifically been designated, but many displaced users chose Amezou as their next home. Amezou Link's existing name recognition played a part, as did the innovative thread-floating format. Some Ayashii World users were already fond of Amezou himself. The Internet population was still much smaller than it is now, and users hungry for places where people gathered flocked to Amezou. Word spread, and the site grew like a snowball.

Like the contemporary 2channel front page, which displays a list of boards on the left and the board itself on the right, Amezou displayed links to boards on the left and the boards themselves on the right. Initially those left-hand links led to external boards, but they soon became links to the many different boards Amezou installed himself. While all 2channel boards use the thread-floating format, Amezou also retained traditional Ayashii World-style boards.

At its peak, with the slogan “Announcements Other Than Those from Imperial General Headquarters,” it was administering dozens of boards.[^tn-ame-daihonei] Some were managed by people other than Amezou, but nearly all were installed by him. As a whole, the site enjoyed a golden age from around late 1998 to May 1999, reaching the summit of the message-board world. At that time, Amezou really was the center of the Internet.

However, increasing user numbers raised server load, and Amezou gradually became sluggish. Trolling increased, and requests to delete material concerning crimes and dōwa issues poured in; there were reportedly threats as well.[^tn-ame-dowa] Amezou increasingly had no choice but to give up managing it. The boards moved from server to server like wandering nomads, but never found a permanent home.

> [3881] At last, even BIG has complained (tears). 
> Poster: Amezou. 
> Posted: Tuesday, February 2, 1999, 18:46. 
>
> Thank you for using our service. Regarding the website you currently operate:
>
> Because its CGI execution rate is abnormally high, the load on the BiG-8 server is currently extremely high and response is poor.
>
> Looking at your website, we see that you use several message boards on the top page. When multiple boards are launched at once on a high-traffic top page, each viewing of that page starts multiple CGI processes simultaneously. This is extremely inefficient for the server: it inconveniences other customers and also amounts to strangling your own site.
>
> *A post by Amezou himself during the BIG-server period.*

Another reason for Amezou's slowness was that displaying board posts had not been separated into HTML as on the contemporary 2channel. Every reading operation invoked and launched a CGI process, placing load on the server; early 2ch worked the same way. However, even modifications to generate HTML did not dramatically reduce the load, and the system could not handle the ever-growing population. Harshly put, Amezou's coding skills alone probably could not cope with that growth. Incidentally, 2channel initially used Perl like Amezou, but soon afterward was implemented in C, said to impose less load than Perl.

At the time, it was normal to make do with free hosting when setting up a board. Ayashii World and others also used free rented boards. With Amezou, however, the number of people posting reached a point where entire servers had to be rented. The tendency grew to rent relatively expensive servers and keep them from going down.

When 2channel began as an Amezou refuge, Amezou users subjected it to the strongest possible abuse. Nevertheless, robust servers that stayed up enabled it to retain users. Its earliest stability was questionable, but it improved sharply after obtaining sponsorship. Other examples include Ayashii World II and Ayashii World @ Mirai, which retained users by renting powerful, expensive WebARENA servers and keeping their boards online.

In that respect Amezou lagged behind. He did try distributing boards across servers, but a service of Amezou's scale imposed financial burdens, and hosting servers in Japan could also encounter legal obstacles. Above all, no provider was carefree enough to take the entire huge Amezou service as it was. Whereas contemporary 2channel at least nominally follows the law and accepts deletion requests, Amezou at the time was, in the source's words, outright vicious: a den of crimes, dōwa disputes, academic-pedigree taunts, extortion, hacking, trolling, and dubious information of uncertain truth. Once a fight began, people could be harassed online and offline until everything about them was exposed. Amezou no longer had the strength to control all those users.

Amezou was a high-school teacher. On underground boards then, hacking servers to steal personal information and using it for threats was commonplace. The information that he was a high-school teacher was itself apparently discovered by people trying to destroy Amezou. There were even rumors that a pressure group went to Kagawa, where he worked. It is easy to imagine the excessive burden on someone single-handedly managing the largest collection of boards not merely in the underground, but across the Japanese Internet.

In June 1999, the Whistleblowing board closed. A prominent theory is that Amezou was threatened over a dōwa issue involving Kyoto city buses, but it might have been someone impersonating such a group; nothing definite can be said, because IP logs were not being kept. Admittedly, there were also rumors that “Amezou collects IP addresses,” and cases in which something resembling IP logs was actually seen. At the time, an anonymous board collecting IP addresses was considered outrageous. 2channel, too, reportedly kept no IP records until 2003, not even Apache-level logs. But with Amezou's destruction, the evidence vanished, leaving no way to verify it.

Around mid-1999, the following wording was added to the front page:

**Defamatory posts and adult information are not accepted.**

The article regards this as unmistakable evidence that Amezou had yielded to some form of pressure. Having somehow survived repeated complaints from hosting providers, the site had now lost the ability to escape. Users who sought free speech without anyone's pressure did not hide their disappointment.

Thus Amezou rushed toward its end. Around mid-1999, as its population peaked, it became unstable and server outages became routine. A provider called Maitai Net supplied a server to support the collapsing site, but it was too late. Even Maitai Net, which had offered hosting with a “bring on the warez and trolls” attitude, could not handle the ferocity of Amezou's users. The Maitai server also began going down frequently, and the provider finally gave up accommodating Amezou. It disabled posting and effectively withdrew. Maitai Net itself then disappeared, as if following Amezou.

Abandoned by its hosting provider, Amezou declined as though tumbling downhill. Trolling could no longer be stopped, criminal acts went unchecked, and countermeasures were always too late. “Die” (死ね) was made a prohibited term, producing evasive spellings such as 氏ね, pronounced the same way. But the prohibited-word list kept expanding, and users seeking freedom migrated en masse to 2channel. Only trolls remained on Amezou. HTML tags left by trolls lay exposed and unattended on the boards, evoking a wasteland after nuclear war.

By around November 1999, Amezou himself had “disappeared” from the Internet. Toward the year's end, without its administrator and amid trolling and outages, the site rapidly deteriorated. The collapse was so fast that there was no time to select a successor. Since this coincided with 2channel's rise, some theories claim that Hiroyuki trolled and destroyed Amezou. However, treating all the threats, trolling, provider complaints, expulsions, and server failures as Hiroyuki's secret work is a stretch.

Perhaps he really did troll it. But now that Amezou's servers no longer exist, establishing the truth is difficult. Still, as Amezou collapsed and its administrator vanished, 2channel's acquisition of the successor's position did have an element of looting during a fire. It is not entirely impossible to understand old users cursing Hiroyuki—who copied everything, including the boards' appearance—as the mastermind. Looking back, though, perhaps 2channel was possible precisely because of Hiroyuki's personality: even after being derided as a plagiarist and the mastermind behind Amezou's destruction, abused in every possible way, and having his personal information exposed, he kept the boards running with a carefree grin. Amezou may have been a little too earnest to operate such a board.

It is ultimately said to have closed two days before 2000 for the complex reasons above, although accounts of the closure date vary. It repeatedly returned and went down, while refuge sites proliferated, so accurate records were not kept. No particular person decided to close it on a particular day; people simply remember the day they could no longer access it. Yet the search for a “post-Amezou” had already begun in mid-1999, and by summer trolls had become established. In its final stage, board-flooding scripts such as the “Amezou Virus” were widespread, and it no longer functioned as a message board. On December 30, 1999, the amezo.com front page went down permanently. The scattered boards had all disappeared completely by the end of 2000 at the latest.

In the article's present, there is Amezou (Provisional), opened by volunteers, and the Amezā group headed by Amezā III. The former is overrun by “yutori” users; the latter has an older user base but is so quiet that merely continuing to exist is something.[^tn-ame-yutori]

It should also be added that 2ch inherited Amezou's successor position partly because sites such as Amezou (Provisional) and Amezā fell into infighting in early 2000, after Amezou's destruction. Criticism such as “Don't just take over the Amezou name without permission” poured in. Their administrators could not respond effectively and quickly lost trust.

Incidentally, the pot on 2channel's front page turned an insult back on itself: Amezou users loathed 2channel as a “spittoon.” Another theory is that they called it a “pot” because fraudulent spiritual sales were rampant there. Amezou itself was also called a spittoon. Amezou the person, however, was a man of character. In later years he worked with Kazuhiko Nishi on producing 1ch.tv. One might say that his good nature again worked against him: he believed its slogan of “creating a kind Internet.” Since 1ch.tv's complete failure, where he is and what he is doing have remained utterly unknown.

## Amezou's own retrospective on Amezou

When becoming involved with 1ch.tv, Amezou uploaded a statement titled “Apology.” The original is no longer accessible, so nearly the entire text is quoted here.

> Apology Regarding Operation of the Former Amezou Message Boards
>
> Letter of apology
>
> To everyone,
>
> October 2, 2001
>
> Amezou
>
> I apologize for the trouble I caused many people through my inadequate management during the final period of the boards. I would like to make amends by working hard to administer and operate the new boards from now on.
>
> Around the time Amezou collapsed, I developed an aversion to the Internet and found it difficult to go online. The reasons included:
>
> ○ The shock of the collapse of the Amezou BBS I had nurtured.
>
> ○ Malicious attacks.
>
> ○ Many supportive emails arrived, but I could not respond to your requests; they accumulated in a vicious cycle.
>
> ○ I was given root access to Maitai Net and managed it through telnet, but my lack of UNIX knowledge meant I was not managing it properly. The server also malfunctioned frequently, perhaps because of crackers.
>
> ○ Guilt over not managing it, among other things, made even contacting people connected with the Internet an enormous psychological burden.
>
> In fact, even after receiving this offer and returning to the Internet, there have been several times when I felt that aversion might return.
>
> I was the one who asked the people at Amezou (Provisional) to take over. I was also the one who enabled redirection from amezo.com.
>
> I am very grateful for the BBSs bearing my name. I am happy to see them as evidence of the support so many people gave me. In truth, however, I have not kept up very well with the current situation.
>
> I'm sorry: I have a bad habit of closing my eyes when I see people talking about me. I will try to pay attention, even a little at a time. I am deeply grateful to everyone who supported Amezou Link.
>
> Amezou Declaration (editor's note: omitted here because it describes 1ch.tv's management policy).
>
> Amezou Q&A
>
> Q: Why are you called Amezou?
>
> A: Because I feel better when it rains.[^tn-ame-name]
>
> Q: Did you write 1ch.tv's scripts?
>
> A: I wrote the scripts to realize many ideas. Staff members helped me with some of the work.
>
> Q: What are your and Nishi's respective roles?
>
> A: Nishi supplied several ideas. His role is producer, and mine is director.
>
> Q: What do you think of “Amezou (Provisional),” “Amezou 2000,” and “Amezā Net 2,” which are associated with you?
>
> A: I am very grateful for the BBSs bearing my name.
>
> Q: What is this [editor's note: an image of Ameneko]?
>
> A: A character modeled on Amezou, devised by the 1ch.tv staff.

This also suggests Amezou's good nature. However, the Internet was no longer the preserve of a small number of people sharing tacit understandings. Some former Amezou users wept, others pretended they had not seen it, and in time it was forgotten.

Logs survive of Amezou struggling to make 1ch.tv succeed. But as the other participants split into factions, fought among themselves, and disappeared, Amezou was left entirely behind. Unable to take effective action, he remained silent and once again vanished into the Internet's darkness.

>
> 7 Amezou, Saturday, 2001/10/27, 13:33
>
> I worked hard posting this Monday and Tuesday, but the abusive trolling was so bad I couldn't keep posting, so I abruptly changed course to writing an IP-restriction script.

>
> Uuuuuu
>
> Aaaa

> I have to keep trying.

> [!info] Cultural context and translator's notes
> “Amezou” names both the administrator and his service; the translation distinguishes them where the Japanese context permits. This retrospective is openly sympathetic to the administrator and harsh toward some users. Its rumors, competing explanations, and statements that evidence has vanished are retained as uncertainties. Descriptions of underground culture, personal-information theft, and legal pressure are the article's historical account. “Residents,” “refugees,” and “successors” describe communities of users and replacement sites, rather than formal institutions. Quoted apologies retain their vulnerability and content, while their unusually frequent Japanese commas have been normalized for readable English. The source's 2014 perspective, including what was then unknown about Amezou, has not been updated.

[^tn-ame-thread]: A thread-floating board moves a thread upward in the thread list when it receives a reply. The article contrasts that design with the earlier style in which posts flowed past on a single board page.
[^tn-ame-chubo]: 厨房 (*chūbō*) is used here as an insult for immature or disruptive users. The article's contrast is between an underground community guarding its norms and newcomers arriving through public links, not necessarily the newcomers' literal ages.
[^tn-ame-daihonei]: “Imperial General Headquarters” translates 大本営. The slogan presents the board as an alternative to official announcements; its historical wording is preserved rather than replaced with a modern political slogan.
[^tn-ame-dowa]: The source uses 同和問題 (*dōwa mondai*) without explaining particular incidents or identifying the alleged pressure groups. The term concerns discrimination against historically marginalized Buraku communities; see the [Japanese Ministry of Justice explanation](https://www.moj.go.jp/JINKEN/jinken86.html). The article's allegations should not be generalized to those communities.
[^tn-ame-yutori]: ゆとり (*yutori*) is the source's disparaging label for younger or supposedly less capable users. It is retained as a community insult rather than translated into a claim about those users' actual education or ability.
[^tn-ame-name]: 雨 (*ame*) means “rain,” making the explanation a wordplay on the beginning of Amezou's name.
